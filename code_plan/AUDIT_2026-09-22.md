## 0. Background — what this project is trying to do, and what "the v8–v10 cycle" means

Skip this section if you already know the project. It exists so the rest of the document
doesn't have to keep stopping to define terms.

**The goal.** This project trains an AI model to invent realistic fake rainfall data. Real
rain gauge records are short (10 years here); training a downstream reinforcement-learning
agent to control a stormwater drainage system needs far more examples of floods and storms
than 10 years can supply. So the plan is: train a generative model on the real 10 years,
then ask it to produce many more years of synthetic rain that behaves like the real thing,
and use that synthetic rain to train the flood-control agent.

**How the generator works, in one paragraph.** The model is a *diffusion model* — the same
family of AI model behind image generators like Midjourney, adapted here to generate a
sequence of numbers (rainfall every 5 minutes) instead of a picture. It is not trained from
scratch: it starts from a **checkpoint** — a file of already-trained weights from a more
general model — and is then **fine-tuned**: given additional, cheaper training specifically
on the Astlingen rain data, so it specializes without having to learn everything about
sequences from zero. One important setting chosen before fine-tuning is the **context
window** (called `seq_len` in the code): how many consecutive 5-minute steps of rain the
model looks at as a single unit while learning and generating. A wider window lets the
model "see" longer storms; a window narrower than a typical storm forces the model to
learn storms in fragments, the same way you'd struggle to learn what a sentence is if you
were only ever shown three words of it at a time.

**"v8", "v9", "v10".** These are successive versions of the fine-tuned model, each trained
with a different context window: v8 sees 24 steps (2.0 hours), v9 sees 36 steps (3.0
hours), v10 sees 64 steps (5.33 hours). Each has two output flavors — a plain version and a
"`_cal`" (calendar) version — which differ only in how synthetic years are stitched together
after generation (explained in §3.1's sibling finding on assembly mode, not repeated here).

**"Gate A".** Before anyone trusts synthetic rain enough to train a safety-relevant flood
controller on it, the project's own rulebook (`code_plan/ACCEPTANCE_CRITERIA.md`) specifies
an exam the synthetic rain must pass: eighteen numerical checks, grouped into five
difficulty tiers (Tier 1: does it rain the right total amount, roughly as often as real
rain does; Tier 2: are individual bursts of rain the right size, including rare heavy ones;
Tier 3: does rain arrive in storms of the right length and frequency, not just the right
total; Tier 4: are the biggest, rarest storms — the ones that matter for flood safety — of
the right size; Tier 5: does the model reproduce the real seasonal and daily rain pattern).
Each check has a pass/fail band. "Gate A tally: 11/18" means the model passed 11 of 18
individual checks.

**Two Tier-3/4 terms worth knowing in advance:**

- **ACF (autocorrelation) and "ACF RMSE".** Autocorrelation asks a simple question: if it's
  raining right now, does that make it more likely to still be raining an hour from now?
  Real storms are "sticky" this way — rain clusters in time rather than appearing as
  scattered, independent 5-minute blips. Autocorrelation measures how sticky, at different
  time gaps (1 hour later, 2 hours later, and so on); "hourly ACF RMSE" is a single number
  summarizing how far off the synthetic rain's stickiness curve is from the real one across
  a whole day of gaps — lower is better, 0 would be a perfect match. A model whose storms
  are too fragmented (comes and goes too fast) will always score poorly here.
- **IDF / return period ("once-in-N-years storm").** Standard hydrology language for "how
  big a storm would you expect to see, on average, once every N years" (see Tier 4 above).
  A "return-period depth ratio of 0.60 at T=10 years" means the synthetic 10-year storm is
  only 60% as big as the real 10-year storm — a shortfall that matters directly for
  designing (or training an AI to operate) flood defences sized for a specific rarity of
  event.

**What this audit is.** The project asked two documents to be written summarizing v8–v10's
results and comparing this work to the academic paper it's built on. This audit independently
recomputes every number in those two documents from the underlying data, to check whether
the documents' claims hold up. In most cases they don't, in specific and fixable ways. This
document is the record of what was found and what to do about it. §5 below tracks which of
those problems have since been fixed in the documents themselves — as of this writing, all 14
identified inconsistencies have been corrected.

## 1. Verdict

**In plain terms:** the project has one genuinely interesting result — making the model
look at a longer stretch of rain at once made its synthetic storms noticeably more
realistic — but that result currently rests on an experiment that changed two things at
once, so it can't yet be trusted as showing what it appears to show. Beyond that one
result, several exams the project's own rulebook requires were never actually run, and two
of them (once run, for this audit) fail badly. None of this means "train more models." It
means: rerun one pair of already-scripted experiments to isolate the one result that
matters, and run two cheap analyses that were skipped.

**Not enough for the thesis yet — but the shortfall is not "more models".**

v10 is a genuine result: expanding the context window from 24 to 64 steps is the first
change in the whole v1–v10 history that moved storm geometry, and it moved it a long way
(storm-count ratio 1.206 → 1.004 against the canonical training reference, hourly ACF RMSE
0.086 → 0.049 — see §0's glossary entry on "ACF" for what that number means). That finding
is real, survives re-verification, and is publishable.

What is not yet defensible is everything around it:

1. **The headline experiment is confounded** — plain terms: to compare context windows of
   24 vs. 64 steps fairly, everything else about the two models should have been identical
   except the window size. Instead, each context length was fine-tuned starting from a
   *different* pretrained checkpoint (a different starting point), and it turns out that
   difference was unnecessary — the checkpoints could have been interchanged. So the
   experiment, as run, changed two things at once (window size *and* starting point), and
   there is currently no way to know which one caused the improvement. This is the single
   most consequential finding in this audit, because it is the project's headline claim.
   Full detail: §3.1.
2. **There is no external baseline** — plain terms: every comparison so far is "this
   version of our model vs. an earlier version of our model." Nobody has run a different,
   independently published rainfall-generation method on the same data to see whether this
   project's approach is actually better than the alternatives, even though the code for
   three such alternatives (`models/KoVAE`, `models/ImagenTime`,
   `models/interpretable_diffusion`) is already sitting in the repository, unused on this
   dataset. The academic paper this project extends *is itself* a comparison of four
   different methods; this project has not yet done the equivalent comparison for its own
   contribution.
3. **Two of the five Gate A exam tiers had never been run** — plain terms: the project's
   own rulebook specifies five categories of checks (§0), but two of them (Tier 4, about
   rare extreme storms, and Tier 5, about seasonal patterns) were simply never computed for
   v8–v10 before this audit. Now that they have been (§4), both come back mostly failing.
4. **Gate B and Gate C have never been run** — plain terms: the project's actual purpose is
   to help a flood-control AI learn from synthetic rain. Nobody has yet tested whether that
   is even true — not by running the flood-control AI itself (that's a later step, Gate C),
   but not even by the cheaper first check of running the synthetic rain through a flood
   simulator to see whether it produces realistic flood behavior at all (Gate B). So the
   project's central claim currently has zero supporting evidence, positive or negative.
5. **The two summary documents assert several things the underlying code and data
   contradict** (§3), including three claims the project's own frozen governance memo
   explicitly forbids making (§5).

The good news: items 1, 3 and most of 5 are cheap to address. Item 3 is already done, in
§4 below.

## 3. Claims in `docs/` that do not survive verification

**In plain terms, before the detail:** this is the heart of the audit. Five specific
things the two summary documents assert turn out, on independent recomputation, to be
either wrong, unsupported, or measuring the wrong thing. None of these are typos — each one
would change a conclusion a reader takes away. They are ordered by how much they matter,
not by where they appear in the documents.

### 3.1 "Different context windows need different starting checkpoints" — false, and it breaks the headline experiment

**In plain terms:** think of a checkpoint as a partially-trained student the project
"adopts" and then teaches the rest of the way. The documents say that to teach a student to
handle a longer stretch of rain at once (a bigger context window), you need to start with
a *differently-built* student — implying the three versions (v8, v9, v10) necessarily
differ in two ways: the lesson (context window) and the student (checkpoint). This audit
checked, and it's not true: all four available "students" (checkpoints) are built
identically as far as this matters, and the training logs prove it by showing that loading
any of them into any context-window setting works cleanly, skipping only the two small
class-name tags that were always expected to be replaced. This means the project could have
kept the same starting checkpoint across all three versions and changed only the context
window — but it didn't, so right now we cannot tell whether v10's improvement came from
the longer context window (the intended experiment) or from starting with a different,
perhaps luckier, initial checkpoint (an accidental side effect). Scientists call this a
**confounded experiment**: when two things change at once, you can't attribute the outcome
to either one alone. This is fixable with two more training runs (§6, E1) that were never
run.

A second, related error: the documents describe the widest context window tested (64 steps)
as a hard ceiling that can't be raised without an expensive, from-scratch retrain. That's
also not correct — the ceiling comes from a small setting in the training configuration
file, not from anything baked into the checkpoint, so it should be changeable essentially
for free. This matters because it reopens an experiment (going even wider than 64 steps)
that the documents had written off as too costly.

**In depth.** `RESEARCH_CONTRIBUTIONS.md` Gap 8 states that mismatched `(checkpoint,
seq_len)` pairs "produce silent shape mismatches", and the 2026-09-21 diary entry states
that targeting `seq_len` 144 or 288 "caused shape mismatches with base weights". The logs
say otherwise. All four runs — including the two that targeted L=144 and L=288 — report:

```
Skipping net.model.map_label.weight        (ckpt=[128, 32] vs model=[128, 4])
Skipping model_ema.modelmap_labelweight    (ckpt=[128, 32] vs model=[128, 4])
Loaded 1006 parameters, skipped 2
```

Exactly two tensors are skipped, both the 4-class conditioning head, in every run. No weight
shape depends on `seq_len`: the grid is always `embedding × embedding = 8 × 8`, and all four
`.ckpt` files are byte-identical in size (54,577,102), which they could not be if their
shapes differed. The L=144/288 runs died on

```
IndexError: index 8 is out of bounds for dimension 3 with size 8
```

which is `DelayEmbedder.ts_to_img` writing past column 8 of a fixed 8×8 grid
(`models/ImagenFew/img_transformations.py:82-86`) — a limitation of the **data transform in
the config**, not of the checkpoint.

Two consequences:

- **The context-length ablation is confounded.** v8←`ImagenFew_24`, v9←`ImagenFew_36`,
  v10←`ImagenFew_64` are three different pretrained initialisations, chosen for a constraint
  that does not exist. The v10 improvement cannot presently be attributed to context length
  rather than to initialisation. This is the single most damaging finding in this audit,
  because the context-window result is the thesis's headline.
- **L=64 is not an architectural ceiling.** Gap 5 calls it a hard maximum requiring a
  "prohibitively expensive" new base checkpoint. The ceiling is `delay × embedding`, set in
  the config. Since no parameter shape depends on the grid and `attn_resolution: [8,4,2]` is
  matched by level (`res = img_resolution >> level`, `networks.py:345`), setting
  `img_resolution: 16, delay: 16, embedding: 16` should give L=256 (21.3 h) while still
  loading 1006/1008 parameters. This needs a one-minute smoke test on the cluster to
  confirm — it cannot be checked locally, as the venv has no torch.

### 3.2 "Chronological holdout eliminates the leakage that inflated v5–v7" — the split is fine, but the exam is graded against the wrong answer key

**In plain terms:** the actual fix here — training the model only on 2000–2007 and never
letting it see 2008/2009 — is done correctly and is a real improvement. The problem is
what happens *afterward*: when scoring how good the synthetic rain is, the evaluation
script compares it against the *entire* 10-year record (2000–2009), 80% of which is the
very data the model was trained on. That's like grading a student's exam using an answer
key that includes questions they were shown the answers to in class — it will always look
more favorable than a fair test. The project's own frozen rulebook says the correct answer
key is the 2000–2007 training years only (used precisely because it's the fair, agreed
reference), and separately warns that the full 10-year numbers are a "sensitivity check,"
not a scoring standard. The new evaluation script uses the sensitivity numbers as if they
were the scoring standard. This single choice changes several pass/fail verdicts, including
flipping one of v10's headline checks from a pass to a fail depending on which reference is
used, and it fully explains why one specific claimed strength (the model matching the
biggest storm ever recorded) turns out to be an artifact of which year's storm gets
compared against which.

**In depth.** `scripts/run_evaluation.py:12` sets `REAL_PATH =
data/rainfall/real_rainfall_data.csv`: the full 2000–2009 record. Every headline number in
`docs/` and in the new diary entry is therefore scored against a reference of which 80% is
the models' own training data, and the 2009 test year is never touched.

The split itself is clean and v8–v10 genuinely never saw 2008/09 — the models are fine. The
*evaluation* is the problem, in two ways. First, `my notes/memo/day_1.md` §3.1 freezes the
**2000–2007 training partition** as "the canonical ground-truth baseline for model
development, statistical screening (Gate A), and holdout comparison", and explicitly
designates the pooled 10-year values (62.3 min, 679.7 storms/yr) as a *sensitivity* result,
not a target. `run_evaluation.py` uses precisely those sensitivity values as "Real". Second,
the verdicts move when the reference moves:

| v10 metric | vs train 2000–07 (canonical) | vs full 2000–09 (as reported) | vs test 2009 (locked) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Volume ratio | 0.915 | 0.915 | 0.982 |
| Storm-count error | **+0.4 %** | +3.0 % | +13.8 % |
| Hourly ACF RMSE | **0.049 (PASS)** | 0.055 (FAIL) | 0.068 (FAIL) |
| Max-burst error | −17.0 % | −17.0 % | **+130 %** |

The extreme-tail narrative is entirely an artefact of this choice. 2009's largest 5-minute
burst is 2.005 mm; the 10-year maximum of 5.555 mm comes from a single event in 2000. Scored
against the locked test year, v10 *overshoots* the maximum by 130 %.

### 3.3 The "biggest storm" comparisons are one lucky (or unlucky) draw, not a real pattern

**In plain terms:** comparing "the single largest 5-minute rainfall spike across 10
synthetic years" against "the single largest spike across 10 real years" is comparing one
number to another number — like comparing the tallest person you happened to meet this year
against the tallest person on record. If you instead look at *every year's* tallest person
(ten real years, ten synthetic years from each model) and average them, a very different
and more trustworthy picture emerges: on that fairer comparison, an earlier, supposedly
worse model (v8) actually matches the real record's typical extreme storm size and spread
better than the model being recommended (v10). The recommended model's celebrated "matches
the record maximum" moment is its single luckiest year, not a systematic strength. The same
logic undoes the stated reason for rejecting another model (v9) — its one weak year isn't
actually unusual; its real, decisive weakness is a simpler and more damaging one: it
generates about 17% too little total rainfall, year after year, consistently.

There's a second problem visible from the same analysis, which no document currently
reports: every model's synthetic years are *too similar to each other* — real years vary a
lot from one to the next (some are dry, some are stormy), but the model's ten synthetic
years vary noticeably less than that. The project's own governance rules already forbid
claiming the model captures real year-to-year weather variability; this is the first
measurement showing by how much that claim would fail if anyone made it.

**In depth.** `FINETUNING_ANALYSIS.md` §3 bolds v10_cal's 5.638 mm maximum against real
5.555 mm as tail preservation, and §5 shows v8 at 4.396 mm as a deficit. Both are
comparisons of one 10-year maximum against another — two draws from an extreme-value
distribution. Split each realisation into its ten constituent years and the ranking
inverts:

| Annual max 5-min burst (mm) | mean ± sd | z vs real |
|---|---|---|
| Real 2000–09 | 3.24 ± 1.12 | — |
| v8 | 3.23 ± 0.55 | **−0.0** |
| v8_cal | 3.52 ± 0.80 | +0.8 |
| v10 | 2.89 ± 0.95 | −1.0 |
| v10_cal | 2.88 ± 1.17 | −1.0 |
| v9 | 2.45 ± 0.22 | −2.2 |

On annual-maximum statistics — the form hydrology actually uses — **v8 is the best model and
v10 is slightly worse**. v10_cal's 5.638 mm is its single luckiest year. The same applies to
"v9 rejected due to a truncated extreme tail (2.83 mm vs 5.555 mm)": 2.83 mm is inside the
observed per-year range [1.92, 5.55], and 2009 itself only reached 2.005 mm. v9's real defect
is its 17 % volume deficit (z = −6.5), which is decisive on its own.

Note also that the generator **under-disperses interannual variability** everywhere, which no
document records: storm duration 2.7 min sd against the observed 4.5, annual max 0.55 mm sd
(v8) against 1.12. `day_1.md` §6 already lists "reproduces interannual climate variability"
as a disallowed claim; this quantifies it.

### 3.4 The training-loss explanation had the right instinct and the wrong mechanism

**In plain terms:** during training, the model reports a single "loss" number every epoch
— roughly, "how wrong its guesses currently are," lower is better. A puzzle in the
documents was that this number gets *worse* (higher) the wider the context window, even
though the wider-window model is clearly producing better rain. The documents explain this
by saying wider-context learning is intrinsically a "harder problem," so a higher loss
there doesn't mean anything is wrong — essentially, "you can't compare test scores between
a harder and an easier class." That explanation isn't what's actually happening. The real
cause is a straightforward accounting bug in how the loss number is computed: it always
divides by the same fixed denominator (64) regardless of how much of the model's working
canvas the context window actually fills, so a wider window's loss looks inflated purely
from arithmetic, not from the problem being harder. Once that division is corrected, the
mystery disappears entirely: the widest-context model (v10) actually has the *lowest*
error of every version compared, and the loss number tracks rainfall quality exactly the
way you'd hope — no paradox, no unfair "harder class." This is a better and simpler
finding than the one it replaces, and should go in the thesis instead.

Two smaller findings came out of chasing this down:

- **A "spectral" (frequency-based) piece of the training loss, presented in the documents
  as one of the project's contributions, was already flagged three weeks earlier — in this
  project's own notes — as a bug that should be switched off, not celebrated.** At the
  widest context window (the one being recommended), this piece of the loss is now
  mathematically provable to have had *zero* meaningful effect on training; it functions
  purely as a fixed, inert rescaling, confirmed to three decimal places against the exact
  theoretical prediction.
- **The "validation" data (year 2008), meant to be used to pick the best training
  checkpoint honestly, is loaded and then never actually consulted.** The model currently
  picks its own "best" checkpoint using the same training data it's learning from — which
  is a bit like a student grading their own homework instead of having a teacher check it
  with a separate answer key. This isn't dangerous by itself, but two documents describe
  this validation step as already happening, and it currently isn't.

**In depth.** `FINETUNING_ANALYSIS.md` §6.3 explains the loss growing with L as the denoiser
"solving a higher-dimensional reconstruction problem", and §6.3.4 as L=24 "achieving low MSE
simply by smoothing out multi-hour transitions", concluding that cross-L loss comparison is
"mathematically invalid". The actual cause is the normalisation. `train_regime.py:343`:

```python
loss = (weight * (w_int * time_loss + fft_weight * fft_loss) * signal).mean()
```

`.mean()` divides by all 64 grid cells while only `seq_len` of them are inside `signal`, so
the reported scalar is proportional to `seq_len/64`. Multiply it back out and the losses are
directly comparable, and v10 is the best on the training objective too:

| | v5 | v6 | v7 | v8 | v9 | v10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| L | 24 | 24 | 24 | 24 | 36 | 64 |
| best loss as logged | 0.0618 | 0.0617 | 0.0620 | 0.0624 | 0.0909 | 0.1556 |
| × 64/L (per active cell) | 0.1647 | 0.1645 | 0.1654 | 0.1664 | 0.1616 | **0.1556** |

This is a stronger result than the "EDM generative paradox" framing it replaces: there is no
paradox, and the correction should go in the thesis instead.

Two related items:

- **The FFT term is presented as a contribution but was already retired as a defect.**
  `docs/FINETUNING_ANALYSIS.md` Innovation 6 offers "2D FFT regularization … analytically
  verified to contribute exactly 1/64 of the time loss". `code_plan/LOSS_FUNCTION.md` §3.1
  derived that same 1/64 three weeks earlier *and* showed the mask is applied in the wrong
  domain (a spatial mask indexing frequency bins), and `day_1.md` §8 item 5 lists
  "Spatial Masking of 2D Frequency Spectra (FFT Loss Bug)" as **permanently retired as
  mathematically invalid**. All three of v8, v9, v10 were nevertheless trained with
  `fft_weight: 1.0` and the bug intact. At L=64 the mask is all-ones, so by Parseval the term
  is *exactly* a 1.5625 % rescale of the time loss — provably inert. Gap 7 ("FFT contribution
  not isolated") can be closed analytically for v10; no ablation is needed.
- **The validation year is loaded and discarded.** `train_regime.py:224` builds `test_ds`
  from `val_years_labelled.csv`; line 237 logs its length; nothing else ever reads it.
  Checkpoint selection is `is_best = avg_loss < best_loss` on the **training** loss. So
  `FINETUNING_ANALYSIS.md` §4.2 — "Validation Partition (2008) … Used for model selection" —
  and `day_1.md` §2.2 — "checkpoint early stopping" — are both untrue of v8–v10. T6.5 is
  still open. `sigma_data` is also still hardcoded to 0.5 (`networks.py:712`) against
  unit-variance `StandardScaler` data, so T6.2 is open too.

### 3.5 Smaller errors

**In plain terms:** these are individually minor slips — a sentence that says the opposite
of what the numbers show, a proposed fix aimed at a cause that doesn't actually exist when
measured, a pass/fail check quoting the wrong threshold, and one description that
undercounts how many rain gauges the data actually comes from. None of them changes the
project's overall direction, but each one would mislead a careful reader if left uncorrected.

- "L=24 covers only 2.0 hours — shorter than the mean storm duration of 62.3 minutes"
  (`RESEARCH_CONTRIBUTIONS.md` Innovation 2). 2.0 h is longer than 62.3 min. The correct
  statement is the one already in `ACCEPTANCE_CRITERIA.md`: the window is *barely twice* a
  storm.
- Gap 1 attributes the volume deficit to the 0.005 mm clip suppressing drizzle. Measured, the
  clip removes **0.00 mm/yr** from every synthetic series (the generator emits nothing below
  the threshold) and 0.02 mm/yr from the real record. The stated cause is not the cause, so
  the proposed fix ("retrain with a softer threshold") targets nothing.
- Gap 10 scores 0.915 as failing a "[0.90, 1.10]" volume gate; 0.915 is inside that interval.
  It does fail the gate that `ACCEPTANCE_CRITERIA.md` actually specifies, which is 0.95–1.05.
- Gap 3 and §8.2 describe a "single gauge". `day_1.md` §2.1: the record is the arithmetic mean
  of **four** gauges (Erft1–4). Spatial averaging smooths extremes and is itself worth a
  sentence in the thesis.
- `results/generated_data/generated_data_log.json` stops at `v8_cal`. v9, v9_cal, v10 and
  v10_cal — the headline models — are unregistered.

## 4. New results: the two exam tiers nobody had graded

**In plain terms, before the numbers:** the project's own rulebook lists five categories of
tests synthetic rain must pass (§0). Two of the five — the one about rare, extreme storms,
and the one about seasonal/daily patterns — were never actually run on any of these models
before this audit. Both turn out to matter a great deal, and both come back mostly failing,
even for the best model. The good news buried in the bad news: the extreme-storm result
gives the clearest, most falsifiable version yet of the project's central hypothesis (that
a wider context window fixes storm behavior) — it shows the fix working exactly up to the
edge of what the wider window can "see," and stopping right there, which is exactly the
pattern you'd expect if the context-window theory is correct.

`ACCEPTANCE_CRITERIA.md` calls this rare-extreme-storm test "the tier that decides whether
the data is safe for flood training at all, and its absence is the biggest hole in the
current benchmark." It took under a minute to compute from data that was already sitting
in the repository.

### Tier 4 — rare, extreme storms ("how big is the once-in-N-years storm?")

**In plain terms:** hydrologists don't just ask "how much does it usually rain" — for flood
safety, what actually matters is the size of the storm you'd expect to see once every 2, 5,
or 10 years, at several different storm lengths (a quick 15-minute downpour, an hour-long
storm, up to a full 24-hour event). This is standard practice in flood engineering, and this
project's rulebook requires it. Nobody had computed it for any of these models until now.
The table below shows, for each model, what fraction of the real once-in-10-years storm
size it actually produces, at five different storm lengths. A value of 1.00 would be a
perfect match; the required passing range is 0.80–1.20.

| Storm length | v7 | v8 | v8_cal | v9 | v10 | v10_cal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15 minutes | 0.94 | 0.82 | 0.90 | 0.48 | 0.81 | 0.91 |
| 1 hour | 0.98 | 0.83 | 0.87 | 0.56 | 0.94 | 0.91 |
| 3 hours | 0.77 | 0.70 | 0.71 | 0.54 | 0.77 | 0.76 |
| 6 hours | 0.71 | 0.69 | 0.71 | 0.58 | 0.79 | 0.79 |
| 24 hours | 0.55 | 0.57 | 0.53 | 0.47 | **0.60** | 0.59 |
| **checks passed (of 15)** | 6 | 6 | 6 | 0 | **7** | 7 |

**Every single model fails this exam overall**, and the shortfall gets steadily worse the
longer the storm duration considered. Three things follow from this:

1. **This is the strongest evidence yet for or against the context-window idea.** The
   recommended model (v10) is nearly exact for 1-hour storms — a duration well inside the
   5.33-hour window it was trained to "see" — and drops to 60% accurate for 24-hour storms,
   far outside that window. That is precisely the pattern the context-window theory
   predicts. The documents claimed v10 "solved storm geometry"; a more accurate and
   ultimately more convincing statement is that it solved storm geometry *up to the edge of
   what it can see*, and left everything beyond that edge unsolved. That's a sharper,
   more testable claim than the one being made, because it comes with a specific
   prediction: widen the window further, and the fix should extend selectively to longer
   storms (§6, E2).
2. **It rules out the simplest proposed fix.** The documents suggest a proposed fix for a
   separate problem (the model's total yearly rainfall running about 9% low) — a single
   across-the-board multiplication to scale everything up. Applying that same fix here
   brings the short storms into the passing range, but 24-hour storms are still only 66%
   of what they should be, still a fail. A single scaling factor cannot fix a shortfall
   that gets *worse* the longer the storm — the two problems have different causes and need
   different fixes.
3. **It's exactly the safety concern the project itself has already raised.** One of the
   documents argues that earlier, worse models would teach a flood-control AI that extreme
   storms are brief and survivable — that the AI would never see, and therefore never learn
   to handle, the kind of storm that actually causes serious flooding. A 24-hour storm at
   60% of its real size does the same thing, on the very model currently recommended for
   exactly that flood-control training.

### Tier 5 — does the model reproduce real seasonal and daily patterns?

**In plain terms:** real rainfall follows a seasonal cycle (wetter in some months, drier in
others) and, in principle, a daily cycle (rain more likely at certain times of day). The
project's conditioning mechanism was specifically designed to teach the model this seasonal
pattern. This check simply measures, using a standard correlation statistic (1.0 = perfect
match, required minimum 0.90 for the seasonal cycle and 0.80 for the daily cycle), whether
the pattern actually comes through in the generated rain.

| | v7 | v8 | v8_cal | v9 | v10 | v10_cal | required minimum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| seasonal-cycle match | −0.36 | 0.44 | **0.91** | 0.11 | **−0.11** | 0.65 | ≥ 0.90 |
| daily-cycle match | 0.23 | −0.02 | −0.50 | 0.02 | −0.28 | −0.11 | ≥ 0.80 |

Only one configuration (`v8_cal`) reproduces the seasonal cycle at the required strength.
**The specific model being recommended overall (v10, using its default output mode) has
essentially no seasonal cycle at all** — a correlation of −0.11 is indistinguishable from
no relationship, or a very weak backwards one. That is a direct problem for the project's
first claimed contribution, which is specifically that its conditioning mechanism teaches
the model where in the year a given block of rain falls. The mechanism clearly *can* deliver
seasonality — v8_cal proves that — but it is not doing so in the configuration currently
recommended. No model reproduces a daily cycle at all, which is expected (nothing in the
conditioning signal currently carries time-of-day information) but was not previously
reported anywhere, and it matters for urban drainage since storms at certain times of day
interact differently with a city's usage patterns.

## 6. What to run, with the hypothesis in each case

**In plain terms:** the rest of this document diagnoses problems; this section is the
to-do list that follows from the diagnosis, in priority order. Each item below is written
as a proper scientific hypothesis with a way to prove it wrong (a "falsifier") — not just
"try this and see," but "here is specifically what result would mean the current headline
claim is wrong." E1 through E3 are cheap (no new model architecture, mostly reruns of
scripts that already exist) and should happen before any further training, because without
them the project's central claim can't be reported honestly either way.

### E1 — Decouple context length from checkpoint *(2 runs, highest priority)*

**In plain terms:** run the missing "control" experiments. Take the context-window setting
that gave the best result (64 steps) and pair it with the *other* starting checkpoint (the
one originally used for the worse-performing 24-step version), and vice versa. If the
64-step window is what actually matters, it should keep working well regardless of which
checkpoint it started from; if the earlier checkpoint is secretly what mattered, swapping
it in should make the wide-window version perform like the narrow one did.

- **H1:** the v8→v10 gain is caused by the context window, not by the pretrained
  initialisation. Then L=64 fine-tuned from `ImagenFew_24.ckpt` reproduces v10's storm-count
  ratio (≈1.00) and ACF RMSE (≈0.049) to within ensemble noise, and L=24 from
  `ImagenFew_64.ckpt` reproduces v8's (≈1.21, ≈0.086).
- **Falsifier:** if L=64-from-ckpt24 behaves like v8, the result is a checkpoint effect and
  the headline claim must be withdrawn.
- **Cost:** two 500-epoch runs at settings already scripted. No code changes — only
  `--model_ckpt` differs. §3.1 shows the checkpoints are interchangeable.
- **Without this, the context-window finding is not reportable.**

### E2 — Push the window past the storm-memory scale *(1 smoke test + 1 run)*

**In plain terms:** if the context-window theory is right, making the window even wider
than 64 steps should extend the "solved" range of storm durations further out — fixing the
6-hour and 24-hour numbers specifically, while leaving the already-fixed short-storm
numbers unchanged. That's a distinctive, checkable prediction, different from what you'd
see if the problem were simply "not enough total rainfall" (which would improve everything
equally, and was already shown in §3.5/§4 not to fix the long-storm numbers).

- **H2:** the Tier 4 deficit is a receptive-field effect. Then a model whose window exceeds
  the accumulation duration brings that duration into band, and extending to L=256 (21.3 h)
  lifts the 6 h ratio from 0.79 and the 24 h ratio from 0.60 toward 0.80+, while 15 min/1 h
  stay put.
- **Prediction that discriminates it** from "the generator is simply short of water": the
  improvement is *duration-selective*. A volume bias correction improves all durations
  equally and fails at 24 h (measured, §4); H2 predicts the opposite pattern.
- **Step 0:** on the cluster, `img_resolution: 16, delay: 16, embedding: 16`, one epoch, and
  check the log still reads `Loaded 1006 parameters, skipped 2`. If it does, Gap 5's
  "prohibitively expensive" claim is void and L=256 is a config change.
- **If the smoke test fails**, fall back to T4.3 (autoregressive block generation) or to
  15-minute resampling, which reaches 16 h inside the existing 8×8 grid.

### E3 — Ensembles, and retire single-draw numbers *(no GPU beyond generation)*

**In plain terms:** generate many more synthetic 10-year records per model (at least 30,
instead of one) so that every comparison is "typical behavior vs. typical behavior" rather
than "one lucky/unlucky draw vs. another," the way §3.3 already showed matters.

- **H3:** several differences currently reported as findings are inside sampling noise.
  §3.3 already demonstrates this for the maxima; ≥30 seeds will confirm how much of the
  v8/v8_cal/v10_cal ordering survives.
- Closes T2.1 + T2.2. Add the moving-block bootstrap band on the observed record (T0.4
  step 5) so "within 2.9 % of real" can be stated against a noise floor — the observed
  interannual sd is 7.8 % on storm count and 8.2 % on volume, so several quoted three-decimal
  ratios are not resolvable.

### E4 — Gate B: route everything through SWMM *(Phase 7, no RL)*

**In plain terms:** feed both the real rainfall and the synthetic rainfall into the actual
flood/drainage simulator the project cares about (with a simple, fixed control policy — no
AI training involved yet), and compare what the simulator says happened: how many overflow
events, how much spill volume, how full the storage tanks got. This is the cheapest test
that actually tells you something about *flooding*, rather than about rainfall statistics
in isolation, and it's the first step toward the project's real goal.

- **H4:** the Tier 4 duration-dependent deficit, not the 5-minute marginal, is what breaks
  hydraulic equivalence. Then v10 matches observed CSO *event counts* (its short-duration
  statistics are good) but under-predicts large *spill volumes* and annual maximum flooding
  volume by roughly the 24 h depth ratio, ≈0.6.
- This is the cheapest test that is non-linear in the rainfall, and it is the first evidence
  that would let the RL claim be stated at all. `ACCEPTANCE_CRITERIA.md` already specifies
  the six KPIs and their bands.

### E5 — An external baseline *(thesis-level gap)*

**In plain terms:** run at least one other published rainfall-generation method
(already sitting unused in this repository) on exactly the same data and the same exam,
so the thesis can say "our approach beats X" instead of only "our newer version beats our
older version." Without this, there is no way for a reader to know whether this project's
specific design choices are actually responsible for its results, or whether any
reasonable generative model would do about as well.

- **H5:** a general-purpose time-series generator with no domain conditioning fails storm
  geometry and Tier 4 by a wider margin than ImagenFew+seasonal-phase conditioning.
- Run KoVAE and DiffusionTS (both already vendored) on the same chronological split, and
  report Discriminative / Predictive / contextFID alongside the physical tiers using the
  existing `logs/TS2VEC/Rainfall_*.ckpt` encoders. Without this the thesis has no comparison
  class, and the base paper's own metrics never appear — which is also the cleanest way to
  substantiate Innovation 3's claim that domain-agnostic metrics miss physical failures.

### E6 — Make the conditioning deliver what it claims

**In plain terms:** figure out whether the seasonal signal the model is supposed to be
learning is actually coming from the per-block "season label" fed to the model, or whether
it's really coming entirely from the separate decision to stitch synthetic years together
in true calendar order (§4's Tier 5 finding suggests the latter). This is a direct test of
whether the project's first claimed contribution — teaching the model about seasons — is
doing anything on its own.

- **H6:** monthly r = −0.11 for v10-Markov versus 0.65 for v10-calendar and 0.91 for
  v8_cal shows the seasonal signal is carried by the *assembly order*, not by the
  conditioning vector. Then conditioning on phase while assembling in random order destroys
  the annual cycle, and the four class embeddings are near-interchangeable.
- **Test:** generate from v10 with a fixed class label and with shuffled labels, and compare
  per-class wet-intensity distributions (`history.csv` already logs `regime_{0..3}_mean/max`
  per epoch — check whether those four series ever separate).
- This bears directly on Innovation 1, the thesis's first claimed contribution.

### E7 — Cheap fixes to land alongside the above

**In plain terms:** small, low-risk code and documentation fixes that should happen
regardless of which of E1–E6 gets prioritized first — none of them require new
experiments, just correcting settings and filling in gaps already identified above.

`sigma_data` 0.5 → 1.0 (T6.2); validation loss computed and used for checkpoint selection
(T6.5); FFT term either taken over the time series without the spatial mask or set to
`fft_weight: 0` and dropped from the claims (T6.1); `results/reference/observed_stats.json`
frozen so the §5 item 12 drift stops recurring (T0.4); old row-based split CSVs deprecated
(T1.1).
