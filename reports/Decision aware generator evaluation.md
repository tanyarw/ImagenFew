# Judge generators by the rankings they keep

Wrap the paper around one question: **when generated scenarios are used to test controllers, which generator errors change the verdict?** If the new experiments support it, your answer is that a generator only has to be right about the *differences* between controllers, not about the rain itself. A score built on those differences should then predict whether generated years rank controllers the way real years do, and standard generative metrics should fail at that. **The principle is not new.** Value equivalence in model-based reinforcement learning, problem-driven scenario generation in operations research, and decision calibration all say "be accurate where the decision depends on it". What six literature searches did *not* find is the specific package. That package has four parts: a score weighted by disagreement between a fixed set of controllers; ranking as the target; validation across dozens of generators; and a testbed where the physics is exact and only the input is generated. Your roughly 28 diffusion variants, three classical baselines and the real-window "oracle" are not the contribution. **They are the test population**: a set of generators with known, different errors. The natural venue is NeurIPS's **Evaluations & Datasets track**, which replaced the Datasets & Benchmarks track in 2026 and now treats evaluation itself as the subject of study ([NeurIPS blog](https://blog.neurips.cc/2026/03/23/introducing-the-evaluations-datasets-track-at-neurips-2026/)). The biggest risk is that rankings barely move. Your working controllers all score within about 3% of the standard rule-based controller on the validation year. A controller trained on rain that gave about 40% less overflow did exactly as well as one trained on real rain. So the first step is the controller-ranking study you have already designed. It needs no training and about 600–1,900 CPU-hours by your own estimate, and it should be run as a go/no-go test.

## Six fields already say "be accurate where it matters"

Your project sits where six research lines meet. Each owns one piece of the headline. **Model-based reinforcement learning** (RL) says a learned model only has to be right for the policies you care about. **Operations research** (OR) says scenarios for a stochastic optimisation problem only need to be right where the costs depend on them. **Forecast verification** has scores that count errors only in high-impact regions, such as heavy rain. **Robotics** judges simulators and learned "world models" by whether they rank robot policies the way reality does. **Decision-focused learning** trains generators against a downstream cost. **Real-plus-synthetic statistics** combines a few real cases with many model outputs to get valid error bars. None of them does what you propose, but together they cover most of the ground.

### The three neighbours a reviewer will raise first

**Value equivalence is the conceptual parent.** Two models are "value equivalent" for a set of policies if they give those policies the same values ([Grimm et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.03506)). Swap "model" for "rain generator" and "set of policies" for "your controllers", and you have most of the headline. Your claim is narrower and weaker in a useful way. Ranking only needs the *gaps* between controllers to survive, not each controller's score. A generator that makes every controller overflow 40% less, in the same way, fails value equivalence but keeps the ranking. Value-gradient weighting also shows the idea helps most when the model has **limited capacity** ([Voelcker et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.01464)). That matches your diffusion model, which spends most of its loss on light rain and very little on storms.

**OR has a formal version of your score already.** Henrion and Römisch measure scenario error as the worst error in expected cost over all feasible decisions ([Henrion & Römisch 2022](https://www2.mathematik.hu-berlin.de/~romisch/papers/HeRo22MP.pdf)). Prochazka and Wallace build scenario sets that reproduce how a fixed **pool of candidate solutions** performs out of sample ([Prochazka & Wallace 2020](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/comgts/v17y2020i2d10.1007_s10287-020-00369-2.html)). That pool is structurally your set of controllers. A stochastic-programming reviewer will say your score is "their distance with a different set of test quantities". The research notes flag the Prochazka and Wallace paper as **the most important one to read in full** before claiming novelty, because its loss might already use differences or ranks.

**Robotics measures exactly your target, but only after the fact.** Kadian and colleagues defined a sim-versus-real correlation over 9 navigation agents. The default simulator scored **0.18**, because some agents learned to slide along walls, an artefact only some policies exploited. Tuning the simulator raised it to **0.844** ([Kadian et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06321)). SIMPLER introduced the **mean maximum rank violation** (MMRV): for each policy, find the largest real gap among the pairs the simulator puts in the wrong order, then average. A near-tie mis-ordered costs little; a large gap mis-ordered costs a lot ([Li et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05941)). SIMPLER also found that wrong mass and friction shifted success rates by up to about 15% yet **left rankings intact**. That is a common-mode error, one that hits every policy the same way. A 2026 position paper argues world models should be judged by policy ranking rather than video quality, but proposes no weighted score and runs no experiments ([Yu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15032)). The gap you can fill is the *prediction*: a score you compute from the generator that tells you, before the real test, whether rankings will hold.

## Most of the parts are known; four gaps survive

Be explicit in the paper about what is **not** new. "A model only needs to be accurate where the decision depends on it" is published in RL (value equivalence, value-aware loss), in OR (problem-driven scenario generation since about 2015), in ML calibration (decision calibration) and in forecast verification (threshold-weighted scores). "Statistical fidelity does not predict usefulness" is well established in images ([Ravuri & Vinyals 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10887)), tabular and relational data ([2024 benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03411)), finance ([CTBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02758)), video world models ([WorldArena](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08971)) and hydrology ([Nguyen et al. 2023](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/b94d5a1b-9633-4492-833c-e2752efa5883)). Judging a simulator by whether it ranks policies as reality does is standard in robotics. Training a diffusion generator against a downstream cost is a crowded 2025–2026 topic. Calibrating a rain generator toward extremes for impact studies is published ([McInerney et al. 2025](https://mssanz.org.au/modsim2025/files/K04.mcinerney.pdf)). The fact that gluing independent blocks loses dependence longer than a block is **textbook**. Block-bootstrap theory gives the bias of a variance estimate from blocks of length ℓ as about −G/ℓ, where G sums the autocorrelations weighted by lag ([Nordman 2009](https://arxiv.org/abs/0903.0474)). Weather-generator researchers call the same symptom "overdispersion": the generator understates how much monthly totals vary ([Katz & Parlange 1998](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/53888)). Finally, "a bias common to all controllers cannot change their order" is close to trivial.

### The idea fits in one line of algebra

Take two controllers, A and B. On each year, compute A's overflow minus B's. Real years give an average gap *g*. Generated years give *g* plus an error *e*. **The order of A and B is kept whenever the error is smaller than the gap.** The error comes only from rain on which A and B behave differently. Rain where they act the same cancels out, however badly the generator draws it. This is why a generator can be 40% too dry and still rank correctly, and why a generator that misses big storms can fail even if its everyday rain is perfect: big storms are where controllers differ. The research notes derive this condition themselves; it was not found stated for scenario generators. It is strictly weaker than value equivalence, which needs each controller's score to match. But a reviewer can fairly call it an immediate corollary of known distance bounds. **So the paper cannot rest on the algebra. It must rest on the evidence that a score built this way predicts ranking agreement across many generators and standard metrics do not.**

## Build the paper around ranking validity

### The claim and the shape of the paper

A working title: *"Accurate where it matters: predicting when generated scenarios rank controllers correctly."* The one-sentence claim: **generated scenarios rank controllers correctly when the generator is accurate on the differences between controllers; a score built on those differences predicts ranking agreement with real held-out years across dozens of generators, while standard generative metrics, overflow-level errors and hand-weighted forecast scores do not.**

The paper has six parts. It opens with the practical problem: testing a controller takes many years of inputs, and the real record has one or two spare years. It states the one-line condition above as a motivating lemma, not as the contribution. It presents the testbed: SWMM, the standard physics-based sewer simulator, on the Astlingen benchmark catchment, where the physics is exact and only the rain is generated. It builds a population of generators with known, graded errors and a population of about 300 controllers. It runs the meta-evaluation: which scores, computed without the held-out years, predict each generator's ranking agreement on them? It ends with the fix: select or train the generator for the region where controllers disagree, and show that ranking agreement recovers.

Agreement is measured two ways. **Kendall's tau** is the share of controller pairs put in the same order, rescaled to run from −1 to 1. **MMRV**, from SIMPLER, penalises each wrong order by the real gap it gets wrong. Your ranking-study design already uses both, plus Pearson correlation for comparability with robotics.

### Your existing work becomes the test population

The frustration that "preprocessing, conditioning and joining changes do not make a paper" is right, and it points to the answer. Those changes are useful precisely *because* they moved window-level scores and barely moved the sewer. That is the paper's evidence that the usual metrics measure the wrong thing. Experiment 8 deserves a word of explanation. **Prediction-powered inference** combines a few real labelled cases with many model predictions, and uses the real cases to correct the predictions' bias ([Angelopoulos et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09633)). It only reduces uncertainty when each real case has its own paired prediction. Unpaired generated years cannot shrink the error bars of an unbiased estimate; they can only trade bias for variance. With pairing, the gain is set by the correlation ρ between real and twin *differences* between controllers. The effective number of real years grows by about 1/(1 − ρ²): about 5.3 times at ρ = 0.9, and only 1.3 times at ρ = 0.5. That links your headline to a number practitioners care about. Two warnings apply. With very few real cases, the tuned version of the method can do *worse* than using the real data alone ([Eyre & Madras 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.12665)). And when the proxy is no better than what it judges, no debiasing method can more than halve the real data needed ([Dorner, Nastl & Hardt 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13341)). Use storm events, not years, as units.

## Rankings may not budge, and other risks

### The counter-evidence is real

The most serious risk is that **generator errors barely change rankings**. In a 2020 water-resources study, the choice of scenarios had "a large influence on the robustness value" of each option but "a relatively small influence on how those decision alternatives are ranked" ([McPhail et al. 2020](https://www.deepuncertainty.org/2020/09/11/impact-of-scenario-selection-on-robustness-2020)). SIMPLER found the same with wrong physics ([Li et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05941)). Your own data points the same way. A controller trained on rain with about 40% less overflow matched one trained on real rain. Your working controllers, and four fixed valve settings, all score between 0.97 and 1.00 times the standard rule-based controller's overflow on 2008, and training seeds of one design differ by 0.02–0.04. With gaps that small, real-year noise may swamp any generator effect. Your design even predicts that the median pair of competitive controllers needs more than 8 real years to separate. If all generators then rank about equally well, the score has nothing to predict.

There is counter-counter-evidence. Different scenario designs did reorder options in another water study ([Quinn et al. 2020](https://essopenarchive.org/users/551389/articles/604354-can-exploratory-modeling-of-water-scarcity-vulnerabilities-and-robustness-be-scenario-neutral)). Storm length reorders sewer controllers ([Huang et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850747/)). A trace-driven network simulator predicted a new streaming algorithm would stall 1.3 times *more* than a rival, when a corrected simulator, confirmed by deployment, showed 1.4 times *less* ([CausalSim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01811)). In your own catchment, the largest 10% of events carry about 54% of the overflow, so a tail-weak generator should hurt controllers that differ on big storms. **The mitigation is experiments 2 to 4**: create flips on purpose, add controllers that really differ, and use a stand-in reality with an exact ranking. And there is a fallback. If rankings truly survive large errors, the paper becomes "common-mode errors are harmless: a generator 40% too dry ranks controllers as well as the record, and here is how many generated years it takes to separate controllers that one real year cannot". That is still an evaluation finding, but a smaller one.

### Your current numbers need re-running

The 0.4 versus 0.9 metric-validity numbers predate the best-checkpoint fix and target overflow level, not ranking (see above). The ranking study itself has not run. One catchment, one validation year (2008) and one spent test year (2009) are a thin truth. Leave-one-year-out across 2000–2008 helps, but most controllers trained on 2000–2007, so those years are not clean. The release also needs checking: the rain comes from a regional water authority's gauges, and the track requires datasets and code to be public by the camera-ready deadline.

### Reviewers will push on novelty and on circularity

Expect "this is value equivalence" and "this is Henrion and Römisch with a different function class". The answer has four parts: an outside-input generator rather than learned dynamics; gaps between a fixed set of controllers rather than each controller's value; a score tested for *predicting* ranking agreement across many generators; and a testbed that isolates generator error. Expect also "why not just rank on the real data?" The honest answer is that one or two real years cannot separate your controllers, and that the score is computed on training-period rain to forecast held-out agreement. But computing it needs controllers run on real storms they may have trained on, so show it holds when those storms are left out. Answer the degenerate-generator trap too: a generator that removes all differences between controllers must score badly, not well ([Lin et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13403)). Report Kendall's tau on the competitive controllers, since weak or collapsed controllers inflate any correlation ([GraphUniverse](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21097)). Finally, the field moves fast. Decision-weighted flow matching, policy-aware simulator learning and the decision-centric position paper all appeared between May and August 2026. Someone could publish your exact idea before May 2027.

The far tail may also be out of reach. A weather model trained without the strongest cyclones could not forecast them ([Sun et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.14932v3)). Standard diffusion models are known to get heavy tails wrong: on a heavy-tailed weather-model field, a tail-shape error score was about 45 for a standard model and about 0.5 with heavier-tailed Student-t noise, where lower is better ([Pandey et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.14171)). If controllers disagree mainly on 100 mm storms, no generator trained on ten years may get them right. Then the paper should say so, and scope the fix to the storms the record does contain.

## Conclusion

The engineering work was not wasted; it was mislabelled. Treated as contributions, the transforms, labels and joining methods are minor variants of known methods. Treated as a population of generators with known errors, they become rare evidence. Few studies anywhere have dozens of generators, hundreds of controllers, exact physics and real held-out years in one place. The finding that most of those changes moved window scores and not the sewer is the paper's best argument that the field measures generators in the wrong currency. The one-line condition, that order survives while the generator's error on a gap stays smaller than the gap, also reframes your most puzzling result. A generator 40% too dry that still trains a controller as well as real rain is not an anomaly. It is what common-mode error predicts.

The decision now is about order, not ideas. Run the ranking study first, because it decides whether there is a flip to predict. If rankings vary, the paired-twin design turns the headline into a practical number, the count of real years a generated set is worth. That would make the paper useful well beyond sewers, for anyone testing controllers on generated weather, demand or traffic. If rankings do not vary, the honest paper is smaller but still publishable: real-world testing of controllers is more forgiving of generator error than the generative-modelling literature assumes. In both cases, the Evaluations & Datasets track is built for this kind of paper.
