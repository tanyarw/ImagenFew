# ML work on task-aware and decision-aware evaluation of generative models, and time-series generation benchmarks with downstream decision tasks

Scope note: the proposed headline is "a scenario generator only needs to be accurate where the decision-makers (controllers or policies) disagree". It would be implemented as a decision-weighted fidelity score, meaning generator error weighted by the difference in outcomes between policies. That score would be shown to predict whether generated rainfall ranks controllers as real rainfall does, while standard generative metrics do not, and it comes with a fix: select or train the generator to be accurate in that region. Every work below is rated against this headline as **SAME IDEA**, **PARTIAL** or **DIFFERENT**. Researched 2026-10-22.

## Q1. Fidelity versus utility: do standard generative metrics fail to predict downstream usefulness?

### Takeaway
Many papers show that standard fidelity metrics (IS and FID; detection or C2ST-style scores; likelihood) predict downstream utility poorly or inconsistently. The utility tested is almost always a supervised ML task (train on synthetic, test on real, or classification accuracy), or in a few cases a trading or retail-energy decision. None of the papers found tests whether a fidelity metric predicts the *ranking of control policies*. The thesis finding (window-level C2ST rank correlation of about 0.4 against downstream outcome, and about 0.9 for day-scale metrics) fits an established pattern, but it is new in its decision setting and in its finding about the scale of the metric.

### Cited Findings
- **Ravuri & Vinyals, "Classification Accuracy Score for Conditional Generative Models" (NeurIPS 2019).** The paper trains classifiers only on generated images and tests them on real images (CAS). It reports that "IS and FID were neither predictive of CAS nor useful for evaluating non-GAN models", and that BigGAN-deep loses 27.9% top-1 accuracy relative to real data even though its FID is strong. **Rating: DIFFERENT.** It is the canonical precedent for "fidelity metrics don't predict utility", but its utility is classification and nothing in it is weighted by a decision. — [NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2019/hash/fcf55a303b71b84d326fb1d06e332a26-Abstract.html); [arXiv 1905.10887](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.10887.pdf)
- **Alaa, van Breugel, Saveliev & van der Schaar, "How Faithful is your Synthetic Data? Sample-level Metrics for Evaluating and Auditing Generative Models" (ICML 2022, PMLR 162:290-306).** It proposes a domain-agnostic three-part metric: α-Precision (fidelity), β-Recall (diversity) and Authenticity (generalisation). Each is estimated by sample-level binary classification, and the paper uses it to audit samples and discard low-quality ones after generation. **Rating: DIFFERENT.** It is task-agnostic by design: "fidelity" means closeness to typical real samples, not accuracy where decisions depend on the data. Its post-hoc sample filtering is a useful contrast to a decision-weighted filter. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/alaa22a.html); [arXiv 2102.08921](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.08921.pdf)
- **Stein et al., "Exposing flaws of generative model evaluation metrics and their unfair treatment of diffusion models" (NeurIPS 2023).** It is a large-scale study of image metrics and documents that FID-style metrics fail, with diffusion models treated especially badly. **Rating: DIFFERENT.** It critiques metrics against human judgement, not against a downstream decision. — [arXiv 2306.04675](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.04675)
- **"All Current Generative Fidelity and Diversity Metrics are Flawed" (position paper, arXiv May 2025).** It builds sanity checks for known failure modes, such as lack of robustness to outliers and unclear lower and upper bounds, and argues that every current fidelity and diversity metric fails some of them. **Rating: DIFFERENT.** It audits generic metrics and has no task. — [arXiv 2505.22450](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.22450)
- **"Benchmarking the Fidelity and Utility of Synthetic Relational Data" (arXiv 2410.03411, 2024).** It correlates a detection-based fidelity score (XGBoost real-versus-synthetic, which is C2ST-like) with ML utility across generators. The correlation is only moderate overall (about −0.54 for one fidelity metric and −0.46 for the other) and varies by dataset; on Walmart the second metric is essentially uncorrelated at about 0.03. When models trained on synthetic data are ranked against models trained on real data, Spearman ranges from about 0.98 for one generator to about −0.43 for another. The authors warn that unweighted rank correlations can mislead and add a weighted Kendall's τ. **Rating: DIFFERENT.** It is direct empirical evidence that C2ST-style fidelity only partly predicts utility, but there is no decision weighting and the utility is predictive ML. — [arXiv 2410.03411](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03411); [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03411v1)
- **TabStruct (arXiv 2509.11950).** Global utility tracks global structural fidelity (Spearman r = 0.84), but local utility is only weakly related to it (r = 0.14). Which utility you measure decides whether fidelity predicts it. **Rating: DIFFERENT.** It is still useful as a "metric scale must match task scale" analogue to the thesis finding that window-level metrics do poorly and day-level metrics do well. — [arXiv 2509.11950](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.11950v2)
- **CoMedBench (arXiv 2608.12805, 2026).** It argues that synthetic medical data can look faithful on aggregate statistics and still fail on a clinically relevant downstream task. **Rating: DIFFERENT.** — [arXiv 2608.12805](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.12805v1)
- **Shoshan et al., "Synthetic Data for Model Selection" (ICML 2023, PMLR vol. 202; first posted on arXiv in 2021).** It tests whether synthetic data ranks *models* the way a real validation set does. Spearman correlations on synthetic data are comparable to those on real validation sets. In harder domains (ImageNet) the paper proposes reweighting synthetic samples to improve rank preservation. **Rating: PARTIAL.** It has the same evaluation target (does synthetic data reproduce the real *ranking* of candidates?) and a "fix" (reweighting). However, the candidates are classifiers, not controllers, and the weighting is not derived from where the candidates disagree. Note: one secondary summary called this "ICML 2021", but the PMLR volume (v202) is ICML 2023. — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/shoshan23a/shoshan23a.pdf); [arXiv 2105.00717](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.00717)
- **Caveats on rank-correlation evaluation that the paper should anticipate.** GraphUniverse reports strong synthetic-versus-real Spearman for model rankings, while GraphWorld reports negative correlations, and the GraphUniverse authors note that including weak baseline models can inflate the correlation. A 2025 LLM-evaluation study argues that rank stability (Spearman) matters more than Pearson accuracy correlation. **Rating: DIFFERENT** (methodological caveats). — [GraphUniverse arXiv 2509.21097](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.21097); [arXiv 2505.08905](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.08905)
- **Lin, Jain, Wang, Fanti & Sekar, DoppelGANger, "Using GANs for Sharing Networked Time Series Data: Challenges, Initial Promise, and Open Questions" (arXiv 1909.13403; IMC 2020 venue not re-verified).** It is a time-series generator evaluated partly by whether downstream predictive algorithms trained on synthetic data are *ranked* the same as when trained on real data (Spearman). The authors find that an autoregressive baseline gets near-perfect rank correlation *despite low-quality samples*, because its outputs have so little randomness that every predictor scores the same. They conclude that rank correlation must be read together with fidelity metrics. **Rating: PARTIAL.** It is the earliest time-series case of evaluating a generator by ranking preservation. Its caveat (a degenerate generator can trivially preserve rankings) is directly relevant: the decision-weighted score must not reward a generator that collapses policy differences. — [arXiv 1909.13403](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.13403)
- **Dumas, Wehenkel, Lanaspeze, Cornélusse & Sutera, "A deep generative model for probabilistic energy forecasting in power systems: normalizing flows" (Applied Energy 2022).** It compares normalizing flows, GANs and VAEs for wind, solar and load scenarios on both *forecast quality* (several complementary metrics) and *forecast value* (an energy-retailer decision case study). **Rating: PARTIAL.** It evaluates scenario generators on decision value and quality side by side, but it proposes no metric that predicts value, and it does not rank policies. The abstract does not state whether quality and value disagreed; that needs the full text. — [arXiv 2106.09370](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09370v3)
- **Stenger et al., "Evaluation is key: a survey on evaluation measures for synthetic time series" (Journal of Big Data, 2024).** It is the main survey of time-series generation (TSG) evaluation measures. A secondary summary says it calls applying the measures to labelled data impractical, so the scope of its utility measures is narrow. **Rating: DIFFERENT** (survey; must be cited as the TSG-metrics reference). I could not retrieve the full text because the Springer pages redirected to a login. — [Journal of Big Data](https://journalofbigdata.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40537-024-00924-7)
- The standard TSG evaluation suite is still the "discriminative score" (a post-hoc classifier separating real from synthetic, reported as |accuracy − 0.5|), the "predictive score" (train-synthetic-test-real one-step prediction MAE with a 2-layer GRU) and distributional alignment. Recent diffusion TSG papers describe this as the commonly adopted core. — [TIMED arXiv 2509.19638](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.19638)

### Inferences
- The finding that fidelity is not utility is well established in images (CAS), tabular and relational data (2410.03411, TabStruct) and medical data (CoMedBench). The thesis cannot claim it as new. What it can claim is (i) the decision setting (ranking controllers in a physics simulator), and (ii) the *scale* finding: window-level C2ST at rank correlation about 0.4 against day-scale metrics at about 0.9. TabStruct's global-versus-local split is the closest analogue found to (ii).
- Ranking preservation as an evaluation target is established (Shoshan et al.; DoppelGANger; the relational benchmark), and so are its pitfalls: degenerate generators, inflation from weak baselines, and unweighted correlations. With about 28 generator variants and only a few controllers, the paper should report a weighted or top-k-focused rank measure as well as Spearman. It should also show that the decision-weighted score cannot be gamed by a generator that removes the differences between policies.

### Gaps
- No paper found tests whether time-series metrics such as discriminative score, predictive score or Context-FID predict a *control or decision* outcome. The search returned no direct critique paper that tests whether these TSG scores predict downstream performance.
- The full text of the Stenger et al. survey could not be retrieved, so its specific recommendations (for example on long series or application-specific evaluation) are unverified.
- The following works should be cited but their links were not re-opened in this session. The links are from prior knowledge and need checking:
  - Esteban, Hyland & Rätsch 2017, TSTR, "Real-valued (medical) time series generation with recurrent conditional GANs" — https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02633
  - Yoon, Jarrett & van der Schaar, TimeGAN, NeurIPS 2019 (origin of the discriminative and predictive scores) — https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2019/hash/c9efe5f26cd17ba6216bbe2a7d26d490-Abstract.html
  - Jeha et al., PSA-GAN, ICLR 2022 (origin of Context-FID) — https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.00981
  - Lopez-Paz & Oquab, C2ST, ICLR 2017 — https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.06545
  - van Breugel et al. 2023, "Synthetic data, real errors: how (not) to publish and use synthetic data", ICML 2023 — link not retrieved.

## Q2. Metrics that weight generator error by a downstream decision, policy or loss

### Takeaway
Three established literatures already formalise "evaluate a distribution only through the decisions it induces":
- **decision calibration** in ML (Zhao et al. 2021);
- **decision-theoretic forecast verification** (Murphy diagrams, and threshold- or outcome-weighted kernel scores such as twCRPS and the threshold-weighted energy and variogram scores);
- **value-aware or value-equivalent model learning** in model-based RL (Q3).

None found defines a fidelity score whose test functions are *outcome differences between competing policies*, nor validates it as a predictor of policy-ranking agreement. That combination is the open space, but the paper must position itself carefully against decision calibration and value equivalence.

### Cited Findings
- **Zhao, Kim, Sahoo, Ma & Ermon, "Calibrating Predictions to Decisions: A Novel Approach to Multi-Class Calibration" (NeurIPS 2021).** It defines *decision calibration*: predicted and true distributions must be *indistinguishable to downstream decision-makers*, meaning each decision-maker's expected loss is the same under either distribution. A predictor is distribution-calibrated exactly when it is decision-calibrated for *all* decision-makers. Restricting to decision-makers with a bounded number of actions gives efficient sample complexity, and a recalibration algorithm improves decisions on skin-lesion and ImageNet tasks. **Rating: PARTIAL (closest formal ancestor in ML).** In effect it is an integral probability metric whose test functions are decision losses, restricted to a decision class. It differs from the headline in four ways: it concerns classifier outputs, not scenario generators; it requires each decision-maker's loss to match rather than only the *differences between policies*; it has no sequential control; and it does not evaluate rankings. — [NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/hash/bbc92a647199b832ec90d7cf57074e9e-Abstract.html); [arXiv 2107.05719](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.05719)
- **Ehm, Gneiting, Jordan & Krüger, "Of Quantiles and Expectiles: Consistent Scoring Functions, Choquet Representations and Forecast Rankings" (JRSS-B 2016).** Every scoring function consistent for a quantile or expectile is a mixture of *elementary* scores indexed by a threshold θ; each elementary score corresponds to a simple economic decision problem. *Murphy diagrams* plot average elementary scores over θ, which shows whether one forecast *dominates* another for every decision-maker and where they differ. **Rating: PARTIAL.** It is the forecast-verification precedent for "compare forecasters by decision-maker-indexed losses, and see where the ranking depends on the decision-maker". It applies to point functionals, not generators of whole trajectories, and is not tied to specific competing policies. — [arXiv 1503.08195](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.08195v2); [scores tutorial](https://scores.readthedocs.io/en/stable/tutorials/Murphy_Diagrams.html)
- **Allen, Ginsbourger & Ziegel, "Evaluating forecasts for high-impact events using transformed kernel scores" (SIAM/ASA J. Uncertainty Quantification 11(3), 2023).** It shows that the threshold-weighted CRPS (twCRPS) is a kernel score. That yields multivariate weighted scores, the threshold-weighted energy score and the threshold-weighted variogram score, for *compound* high-impact events built from several moderate events. The case study is daily precipitation accumulations, focused on events that could cause flooding. **Rating: PARTIAL.** It weights distributional error toward an outcome region of interest, and the application is precipitation for flooding. The weight is chosen a priori by the user (a threshold), not derived from the disagreement between policies. A decision-weighted score could be framed as a transformed kernel score whose transformation is the policy-outcome-difference map. — [arXiv 2202.12732](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.12732); [scoringrules twCRPS](https://scoringrules.readthedocs.io/en/latest/generated/scoringrules.twcrps_ensemble.html)
- **Allen et al., "Weighted verification tools to evaluate univariate and multivariate forecasts for high-impact weather events" (arXiv 2209.04872).** It is a companion review of weighted verification tools, including outcome-weighted CRPS and vertically re-scaled CRPS. **Rating: PARTIAL** (same reasoning as above). — [arXiv 2209.04872](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.04872)
- **Diff2SP, Sun & Liu, "Diffusion Models for Correlated Scenario Generation in Stochastic Programming" (arXiv 2606.05649, June 2026).** It builds the stochastic optimisation into diffusion training so that scenarios are "decision-aware". It claims regret bounds that *link distributional accuracy to decision quality*, and reports gains in both statistical fidelity and downstream optimisation on power-system data. **Rating: PARTIAL.** It gives a theoretical link from distributional error to decision regret, but for a single optimisation problem, with no comparison of policies and no evaluation metric for choosing among generators. — [arXiv 2606.05649](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05649)
- **Xie, Wu, Chen, Su & Chen, "Decision-Weighted Flow Matching for Contextual Stochastic Optimization" (DW-FM, arXiv 2606.16790, June 2026).** It argues that generative scenario models trained for uniform distributional fit are misaligned with decision regret. It reweights the flow-matching velocity-regression loss by decision-sensitive endpoint information, links regret to pathwise velocity mismatch with regret guarantees, and reports lower regret on CVaR benchmarks (portfolio, financial and traffic). **Rating: PARTIAL (closest "fix-side" analogue).** It literally weights generator error by decision sensitivity during training. However, the weight comes from one optimiser's regret, not from the disagreement *between* policies; there is no evaluation metric for ranking generators; and it uses no RL or control simulator. — [arXiv 2606.16790](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16790)

### Inferences
- **Overall novelty verdict for the headline (based on Q2 and Q3).**
  - The *intuition* "a model or generator only needs to be accurate where it matters for the decision" is **already published** in at least four places: problem-driven scenario generation in OR (Q3), value-aware and value-equivalent model learning in MBRL (Q3), decision calibration (Zhao et al. 2021), and weighted scoring rules and Murphy diagrams in forecast verification.
  - The more specific claim has not been found. That claim combines three parts: (a) the relevant region is *where competing policies' outcomes differ*; (b) a fidelity score built on this predicts whether generated scenarios *rank policies as reality does*, while standard generative metrics do not; (c) this is shown on an exogenous-input time-series generator feeding a fixed physics simulator.
  - The paper should present itself as a *ranking-specific, policy-contrast* specialisation of decision calibration and value equivalence, plus an empirical study, not as a new principle.
- A formal hook the writer could use (this is my own derivation, not taken from a source). Let J(π; x) be the outcome of policy π on scenario x, P the real scenarios and Q the generated ones, and d_{ππ'} = E_P[J(π) − J(π')] the true gap between two policies.
  - The headline score is an IPM over the test functions f_{ππ'}(x) = J(π; x) − J(π'; x).
  - If |E_P[f_{ππ'}] − E_Q[f_{ππ'}]| < |d_{ππ'}|, the generated scenarios rank π and π' the same way reality does.
  - This condition is strictly weaker than value equivalence or decision calibration, which require E_P[J(π)] = E_Q[J(π)] for each π separately. It explains why the generator "only needs to be accurate where policies disagree": error in regions where all policies behave identically cancels in f_{ππ'}.
- Weighted scoring rules (twCRPS and its kernel generalisation) already evaluate *precipitation* forecasts with flood-relevant weights. A hydrology or atmospheric-science reviewer will expect a comparison against a threshold-weighted energy or variogram score, as a "hand-chosen weight" baseline against the "policy-derived weight".

### Gaps
- No ML paper found proposes a "task-aware FID" or a decision-weighted IPM for *evaluating* generators (as opposed to training them). It may exist under different wording, but searches did not surface one.
- Decision calibration has follow-ups (for example on omniprediction and calibration for many decision-makers) that were not searched in this session.
- Gneiting & Raftery's 2007 link between proper scoring rules and decision problems is a standard citation but was not retrieved here.

## Q3. Decision-focused learning, value-aware model learning and policy-ranking evaluation: which works evaluate generators this way, and which rank multiple policies?

### Takeaway
Decision-focused and value-oriented work trains generators (or dynamics models) *for* a decision. The 2025-2026 wave applies diffusion and flow models to scenario generation (Gen-DFL, Diffusion-DFL, DW-FM, Diff2SP, decision-focused grid-dispatch scenarios), but it reports regret for one optimiser rather than policy rankings. Separately, robotics and sim-to-real work evaluates simulators and *generative world models* by whether they rank policies as reality does (SRCC, MMRV, Pearson), but without a decision-weighted fidelity diagnostic. Model-based RL supplies the closest theory: objective mismatch, VAML, value equivalence and VaGraM. The thesis sits at the intersection of these strands.

### Cited Findings
**Decision-focused or value-oriented generator training (the "fix" side)**
- **Wang, Chen, Liang, Fioretto & Zhu, "Gen-DFL: Decision-Focused Generative Learning for Robust Decision Making" (ICLR 2026; arXiv 2502.05468).** It replaces point-prediction decision-focused learning with a generative model of the optimisation parameters, samples from the tails of the learned distribution, and claims better worst-case bounds; experiments cover scheduling and logistics. **Rating: PARTIAL.** It trains the generator for the decision, but evaluates by decision loss under one optimiser, with no policy ranking and no metric that predicts it. — [ICLR 2026](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/5c25c15b5b2fd386ab188a918e54c7d5-Abstract-Conference.html); [arXiv 2502.05468](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.05468v1)
- **"Diffusion-DFL: Decision-focused Diffusion Models for Stochastic Optimization" (arXiv 2510.11590, 2025).** This is a decision-focused diffusion model; details beyond the title were not verified in this session. **Rating: PARTIAL** (fix side only). — [arXiv 2510.11590](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.11590v1)
- **Zhou, Zhou, Morstyn & Wang, "Decision-Focused Scenario Generation and Selection for Efficient and Robust Grid Dispatch" (arXiv 2607.05830, July 2026).** It trains VAE, GAN and diffusion scenario generators against downstream operating cost instead of fit to historical data. It adds a differentiable selector that picks decision-relevant scenarios from a generated pool, and reports 0.80-2.02% cost reductions over accuracy-oriented training. It argues that accuracy-oriented scenarios can be "statistically plausible but suboptimal for downstream operation", but this is a motivating claim, not a measured fidelity-against-value correlation. **Rating: PARTIAL.** Its "select scenarios that matter for the decision" is close to the headline's fix, but it uses one dispatch problem, has no competing policies, and has no evaluation metric. — [arXiv 2607.05830](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05830)
- **Diffusion Scenario Tree (arXiv 2509.14832, 2025).** It builds multistage scenario trees from diffusion forecasters and reports gains over MPC and RL baselines on a real decision problem. **Rating: DIFFERENT.** The generator is used for decisions, but it is not evaluated as a ranker of policies. — [arXiv 2509.14832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.14832)
- **DW-FM and Diff2SP** (see Q2). **Rating: PARTIAL.**
- **Fairbrother, Turner & Wallace, "Problem-driven scenario generation: an analytical approach for stochastic programs with tail risk measure" (Mathematical Programming 191, 2022; online 2019).** It contrasts *distribution-driven* scenario generation (capturing the uncertainty well in a probabilistic sense) with *problem-driven* generation that uses the problem's structure. Under a tail-risk measure such as CVaR only the loss tail matters, so scenarios are concentrated in the region that determines the tail, with conditions for consistency with sampling; the result is better and more stable portfolio solutions than Monte Carlo. **Rating: PARTIAL, and the closest statement of the core intuition** ("accurate only where the decision depends on it"). It covers a single stochastic programme, not disagreement between policies, not ML generators and not ranking. It must be cited, and the OR line it starts (Kaut & Wallace on evaluating scenario generators by in- and out-of-sample stability) is probably covered by another researcher. — [arXiv 1511.03074](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.03074); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10107-019-01451-7)

**Model-based RL: decision-aware model learning (closest theory)**
- **Lambert, Amos, Yadan & Calandra, "Objective Mismatch in Model-based Reinforcement Learning" (L4DC 2020).** It shows experimentally that the one-step predictive likelihood of a dynamics model "is not always correlated with control performance". Models that work for a task need not be globally accurate, and vice versa. It proposes re-weighting model training as a first mitigation. **Rating: PARTIAL (strong empirical analogue).** It is the same "standard model-fit metric doesn't predict the decision outcome" result, but for a learned *dynamics* model with one controller, not an exogenous-input generator ranking several controllers. — [arXiv 2002.04523](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.04523); [L4DC](https://mlanthology.org/l4dc/2020/lambert2020l4dc-objective)
- **Grimm, Barreto, Singh & Silver, "The Value Equivalence Principle for Model-Based Reinforcement Learning" (NeurIPS 2020).** Two models are *value equivalent* with respect to a set of policies Π and functions V if they produce the same Bellman updates. A model should be judged by this, not by next-state accuracy. Enlarging Π and V shrinks the equivalence class until only the true model remains. The paper describes its premise as similar to Farahmand's VAML. **Rating: PARTIAL, and the closest conceptual precedent.** "A model only needs to be right for the policies you care about" is VE with Π = the candidate controllers. The headline differs because ranking needs only the *differences* between policies to be preserved, a strictly weaker condition than VE, and because the headline is an evaluation diagnostic for an exogenous-input generator rather than a principle for learning dynamics. — [arXiv 2011.03506](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.03506); [NeurIPS 2020](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2020/poster/18779)
- **Grimm et al., "Proper Value Equivalence" (NeurIPS 2021).** It refines VE; the search listed it alongside arXiv 2106.10316, and the title-to-link mapping should be checked. **Rating: PARTIAL.** — [arXiv 2106.10316](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.10316v1)
- **Voelcker, Liao, Garg & Farahmand, "Value Gradient weighted Model-Based Reinforcement Learning" (VaGraM, ICLR 2022 spotlight).** It rescales the model's MSE loss by the gradient of the current value estimate, so model error is weighted by how much it changes the value. It matches maximum likelihood with large models and outperforms it with limited capacity or distracting state dimensions. **Rating: PARTIAL.** Weighting model error by outcome sensitivity is the training-side twin of a decision-weighted fidelity score; the differences are that it uses one value function rather than the contrast between policies, and that it is used for training, not for evaluating or selecting generators. The limited-capacity result also parallels the thesis lesson on finite capacity and extremes. — [arXiv 2204.01464](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.01464); [ICLR 2022](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2022/poster/6704)
- **"A Unified View on Solving Objective Mismatch in Model-Based Reinforcement Learning" (arXiv 2310.06253).** It surveys decision-aware model learning (value-aware, policy-aware, and others); venue not verified. **Rating: DIFFERENT** (survey to cite for the MBRL lineage). — [arXiv 2310.06253](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.06253)

**Evaluating simulators or generative world models by policy ranking (the evaluation target)**
- **Kadian et al., "Sim2Real Predictivity: Does Evaluation in Simulation Predict Real-World Performance?" (IEEE RA-L 2020).** It defines the Sim-vs-Real Correlation Coefficient (SRCC), the correlation between simulated and real performance of the same agents, using 9 navigation models in a 3D-scanned lab. The SRCC of Habitat as used in the CVPR19 challenge was only 0.18 for success, mainly because agents exploited simulator quirks such as wall-sliding. Tuning simulator parameters raised it to 0.844. **Rating: PARTIAL.** It asks exactly "does the synthetic environment rank policies as reality does?", and the main failure is policy-specific exploitation of simulator error, which is the "error where policies differ" mechanism. However, the simulator is a physics and rendering engine, not a learned generator, and no decision-weighted fidelity score is proposed. — [arXiv 1912.06321](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.06321); SRCC described as Pearson on paired metrics, with the limitation of linearity, in [review arXiv 2111.00956](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.00956)
- **Li et al., SIMPLER, "Evaluating Real-World Robot Manipulation Policies in Simulation" (CoRL 2024).** It uses Mean Maximum Rank Violation (MMRV, lower is better) together with Pearson r to measure whether simulated and real relative policy performance agree; the SimplerEnv repository ships `mean_maximum_rank_violation`. The AutoEval paper (arXiv 2503.24278) reports that SIMPLER gives a better signal than validation MSE but is unreliable across policies, because "different policies may suffer differently from the remaining sim-to-real gap". **Rating: PARTIAL.** It supplies a ready-made rank-agreement metric (MMRV), and the AutoEval observation is the headline's mechanism in robotics language. — [arXiv 2405.05941](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.05941); [SimplerEnv code](https://github.com/allenai/SimplerEnv); [AutoEval arXiv 2503.24278](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.24278)
- **Li, Zhu, Wen, Shen & Xu, "WorldEval: World Model as Real-World Robot Policies Evaluator" (arXiv 2505.19017, May 2025).** A video-generation world model (Policy2Vec) is used to evaluate and rank policies and checkpoints; the authors report a "strong correlation" with real-world performance. A secondary review gives Pearson about 0.942 and MMRV about 0.044 across four policies, but these numbers are not on the abstract page and need checking. **Rating: PARTIAL.** It is a *generative model* evaluated by policy-ranking agreement, which is the headline's evaluation target, but it offers no diagnostic of *where* the generator must be accurate and no comparison against standard video-generation metrics. — [arXiv 2505.19017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.19017)
- **WorldGym (world model as an environment for policy evaluation, 2025).** Per an aggregator listing, policy success rates in WorldGym show a Pearson correlation of 0.78 with real-world success. This is a secondary source and should be verified. **Rating: PARTIAL.** — [Lacuna listing](https://lacuna.tiptreesystems.com/work/worldgym-world-model-as-an-environment-for-policy-evaluation/wrk_8cc42ec42bd6f01285de614b9f6e7116); related project page [world-model-eval.github.io](https://world-model-eval.github.io/abstract)
- **Interactive World Simulator (arXiv 2603.08546, 2026)** and **RoboWorld (arXiv 2607.01060, 2026)** both report correlation between world-model and real-world policy evaluation (4 policies × 4 tasks for the former; RoboArena episodes for the latter). A 2026 survey of robotic video world models notes that Pearson and MMRV are the standard metrics here. **Rating: PARTIAL.** — [arXiv 2603.08546](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.08546); [arXiv 2607.01060](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.01060); [survey arXiv 2601.07823](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.07823)
- **Fu, Norouzi, Nachum, Tucker et al., "Benchmarks for Deep Off-Policy Evaluation" (ICLR 2021).** It is a benchmark that separates estimating a policy's value from *policy selection*, scored by rank correlation; model-based estimators (learned models) are among those compared. A secondary review reports that the model-based autoregressive estimator reaches the highest rank correlation on some tasks (for example 0.91 on cartpole swingup), but this is unverified. **Rating: PARTIAL.** It supplies standard policy-ranking metrics; regret@k is also commonly attributed to this benchmark but was not confirmed here. — [arXiv 2103.16596](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16596); [code](https://github.com/google-research/deep_ope)

### Inferences
- Five strands each hold part of the headline:
  - *standard metrics fail to predict decisions*: objective mismatch, CAS;
  - *accuracy only matters where decisions depend on it*: problem-driven scenario generation, VE, VAML, VaGraM, DW-FM;
  - *evaluate the synthetic environment by policy ranking*: SRCC, SIMPLER/MMRV, WorldEval, OPE benchmarks, Shoshan et al.;
  - *decision-indexed evaluation of distributions*: decision calibration, Murphy diagrams;
  - *train or select the generator for the decision*: Gen-DFL, decision-focused grid scenarios, DW-FM.
- No paper found combines them into a *diagnostic* that (i) weights generator error by between-policy outcome differences, (ii) is validated across many generator variants as a predictor of rank agreement with reality, and (iii) beats standard generative metrics at that prediction.
- The robotics world-model papers (2025-2026) are the most likely reviewers' comparison point for "generative model as a policy evaluator". The paper should state that those works measure rank agreement but do not explain or predict it from generator properties.
- The MBRL literature shows the principle helps most when the model has limited capacity (VaGraM). That supports the thesis framing that a diffusion generator with finite capacity should spend it where controllers disagree, which is also the region of extreme rainfall that drives sewer overflow.

### Gaps
- Farahmand, Barreto & Nikovski, "Value-Aware Loss Function for Model-based Reinforcement Learning" (AISTATS 2017) was not retrieved directly. The link is from prior knowledge and needs checking: https://proceedings.mlr.press/v54/farahmand17a.html. Its loss is effectively a supremum over value functions of the difference in expected next-state value, an IPM with value test functions, which makes it a key citation.
- Donti, Amos & Kolter, "Task-based end-to-end model learning in stochastic optimization" (NeurIPS 2017; from memory: https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.04529) and the Mandi et al. decision-focused learning survey (JAIR 2024; from memory: https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13565) were not re-verified here.
- None of the decision-focused generator papers found evaluates whether generated scenarios preserve the *ranking of multiple policies*; all report regret or cost for one optimiser.

## Q4. Time-series generation benchmarks: do any include a downstream control, RL or decision task, a physics simulator, or horizons beyond the training window?

### Takeaway
The mainstream TSG benchmarks (TSGBench, TSGM, the ImagenTime and ImagenFew evaluation suites) score fidelity, diversity and predictive or train-synthetic-test-real usefulness; none found includes a control or RL task or a physics simulator. The only TSG benchmark found with a downstream *decision* task is CTBench (crypto statistical-arbitrage trading, 2025-2026), and it reports trade-offs between statistical fidelity and profitability. No benchmark found evaluates generation over horizons longer than the training window, for example by stitching windows.

### Cited Findings
- **Ang, Huang, Bao, Tung & Huang, "TSGBench: Time Series Generation Benchmark" (PVLDB 17, VLDB 2024).** It covers ten datasets, ten methods and twelve measures, grouped as vanilla measures, new distance-based assessments and visualisation. A Domain-Adaptation-based generalisation test is the only test beyond fidelity. Statistical analysis of rankings shows "varying performance across different datasets and measures". "Usefulness" means predictive tasks. **Rating: DIFFERENT.** It has no decision or control task and no long-horizon test. — [arXiv 2309.03755](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.03755); [PVLDB](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol17/p305-huang.pdf)
- **Ang, Wang, Huang, Bao, Xi, Tung, Jin & Huang, "CTBench: Cryptocurrency Time Series Generation Benchmark" (arXiv 2508.02758; a PDF appears in the ICLR 2026 proceedings).** It covers 452 tokens, 13 metrics in 5 dimensions (forecasting accuracy, rank fidelity, trading performance, risk assessment, computational efficiency), and 8 models across 4 market regimes. It pairs a Predictive Utility task with a **Statistical Arbitrage** task that checks whether generated series support mean-reverting trading signals, and it reports "trade-offs between statistical fidelity and real-world profitability". It argues that TSGBench does not test practical viability in finance. **Rating: PARTIAL.** It is the only TSG benchmark found with a downstream decision task and it reports disagreement between fidelity and decision utility. However, the decision is a fixed trading rule rather than a set of competing controllers in a simulator, and there is no decision-weighted metric. — [arXiv 2508.02758](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02758); [ICLR 2026 PDF](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/file/9b905031125e56a557db38dff4fa8d21-Paper-Conference.pdf)
- **Nikitin, Iannucci & Kaski, "TSGM: A Flexible Framework for Generative Modeling of Synthetic Time Series" (NeurIPS 2024 Datasets & Benchmarks).** It is an open-source framework covering generative models, probabilistic methods, simulation-based methods and augmentation. Evaluation axes are similarity, downstream effectiveness, predictive consistency, diversity, fairness and privacy. **Rating: DIFFERENT.** Its "downstream effectiveness" is ML-task utility; no control or decision task was found in the abstract-level material. — [arXiv 2305.11567](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11567); [NeurIPS D&B](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/e92bb8c76d1aa420171f51495ce56eaf-Abstract.html); [code](https://github.com/AlexanderVNikitin/tsgm)
- **Naiman, Berman, Pemper, Arbiv, Fadlon & Azencot, "Utilizing Image Transforms and Diffusion Models for Generative Modeling of Short and Long Time Series" (ImagenTime, NeurIPS 2024).** It maps series to images (delay embedding, STFT) and evaluates unconditional generation, interpolation and extrapolation on short, long and ultra-long sequences. It reports a mean 58.17% improvement in short discriminative score over earlier diffusion models, plus "(ultra-)long classification scores". **Rating: DIFFERENT.** There is no downstream decision task, and "long" means long training sequences, not generation beyond the training window. — [arXiv 2410.19538](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19538)
- **Gonen, Pemper, Naiman, Berman & Azencot, "Time Series Generation Under Data Scarcity: A Unified Generative Modeling Approach" (ImagenFew, NeurIPS 2025).** It is a diffusion model pretrained on heterogeneous datasets, with dynamic convolutions for varying channel counts and dataset-token conditioning, and it claims state of the art in few-shot and full-data settings. A 2026 follow-up (TimeMoDE, arXiv 2606.15172) benchmarks against it with c-FID and discriminative score. **Rating: DIFFERENT.** Its evaluation is fidelity-only and it has no decision task. — [arXiv 2505.20446](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.20446); [NeurIPS 2025](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/115993); [code](https://github.com/azencot-group/ImagenFew); [TimeMoDE arXiv 2606.15172](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.15172)

### Inferences
- Adding a physics-simulator decision task (SWMM with RL and rule-based controllers) would be a genuine benchmark contribution; CTBench is the only precedent found, and it is financial and rule-based.
- Because the thesis backbone is ImagenFew, the paper can state directly that the ImagenFew and ImagenTime evaluation suites (discriminative score, c-FID and similar) are exactly the window-level metrics it shows to be weak predictors of the decision outcome.
- No benchmark found evaluates generation beyond the training window (multi-window stitching to day or season scale). The thesis results on windows from 2 hours to 1 day and on joining methods therefore fill a gap; TSGBench and ImagenTime evaluate within the window.

### Gaps
- The Synthcity time-series utility metrics were not verified here. From prior knowledge: Qian, Cebere & van der Schaar, NeurIPS 2023 Datasets & Benchmarks, https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07573. They are believed to be train-synthetic-test-real style, with no control task.
- The exact sequence lengths and metric list for ImagenTime and ImagenFew were not read from the full text.
- I found no 2025-2026 "GenBench"-style time-series generation benchmark beyond CTBench. Searches for long-horizon or beyond-window TSG evaluation returned nothing specific.

## Q5. Synthetic exogenous inputs (weather, demand, prices, traffic) for training and evaluating RL or control: does anyone test whether generated inputs rank controllers correctly?

### Takeaway
Synthetic or adversarial exogenous inputs are widely used to *train or stress-test* controllers (building HVAC under extreme weather, grid topology under LLM-generated extreme weather). No paper found checks whether a *generator of exogenous inputs* reproduces the *ranking of controllers* obtained on real inputs. The nearest analogues come from networking (trace-driven simulation that ranks ABR streaming algorithms wrongly unless de-biased) and robotics (sim and world-model policy ranking, Q3).

### Cited Findings
- **Alomar et al., "CausalSim: A Causal Framework for Unbiased Trace-Driven Simulation" (arXiv 2201.01811; NSDI 2023 per MIT coverage, venue not confirmed in sources).** Trace-driven simulators replay recorded exogenous traces (network throughput) to evaluate new control algorithms (adaptive bitrate, ABR). Traces are biased by the algorithms that collected them. On more than ten months of real Puffer streaming data, a standard expert simulator predicted that a new ABR variant would stall about 1.3× *more* than a competitor, whereas CausalSim, validated by real deployment, predicted about 1.4× *less*. **Rating: PARTIAL.** It is the clearest case found of an exogenous-input simulator *getting the controller ranking wrong*, checked against reality. It fixes the problem by causal de-biasing, not by decision-weighted fidelity, and the inputs are replayed traces, not a generative model. — [arXiv 2201.01811](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.01811); [MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2023/researchers-create-tool-accurately-simulating-complex-systems-0504)
- **Jang et al., "Active Reinforcement Learning for Robust Building Control" (arXiv 2312.10289; Berkeley tech report EECS-2023-101).** It generates hand-crafted extreme-weather environments to train robust HVAC RL agents, then evaluates them on 120 sampled US Typical Meteorological Year (TMY) weather years in Sinergym. **Rating: DIFFERENT.** Generated weather is used to make controllers robust, with no test of whether the generated weather ranks controllers as real weather does. — [arXiv 2312.10289](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.10289)
- **"Weather-Aware Reinforcement Learning for Power Grid Topology Control Under Extreme Events" (IET Smart Grid, 2026).** It uses LLM-generated extreme-weather scenarios to stress-test RL topology control on Grid2Op (IEEE 118-bus). The synthetic scenarios were harder than a scaling-based baseline, with mean do-nothing survival about 11.7% lower in extreme categories. **Rating: DIFFERENT.** It is a stress test with no validation of ranking fidelity against real events. — [IET Smart Grid](https://ietresearch.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1049/stg2.70099?af=R)
- **Dumas et al. 2022** (see Q1): an energy-retailer decision is used to value generated wind, solar and load scenarios. It covers one decision procedure and does not rank controllers. **Rating: PARTIAL.** — [arXiv 2106.09370](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09370v3)

### Inferences
- Using generated exogenous inputs (rainfall) to rank RL and rule-based controllers *against real held-out years*, and explaining rank agreement from where the generator is accurate, appears unaddressed in the control-for-energy, building and water literature found here. CausalSim and Sim2Real predictivity show that ranking failures are real and consequential, which supports the motivation.
- The 2026 grid-control paper shows "harder synthetic weather" is often taken as good in itself. The headline's view is that harder scenarios matter only insofar as they shift the comparison between policies, and that is a useful contrast to draw.

### Gaps
- Input-driven MDPs (Mao et al., ICLR 2019) and the RL benchmarks CityLearn, Sinergym and Grid2Op were not searched in depth, so their use of synthetic exogenous inputs for policy *evaluation* is unverified.
- No hydrology or urban-drainage RL paper (for example on SWMM-based RL control) was searched here; that is presumably covered by another researcher.
- GenCast (Price et al., Nature 2024; arXiv 2312.15796, from memory) is believed to evaluate a generative weather ensemble on downstream tasks (regional wind power, cyclone tracks, relative economic value). This was not verified in this session and should be checked before citing.
