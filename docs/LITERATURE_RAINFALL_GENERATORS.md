# Our diffusion rainfall fills a narrow gap

*Literature review, written 2026-10-01 from the four note files in `research_notes/Rainfall generator literature review/`. A companion note on making long sequences by continuing short pieces is at `docs/LITERATURE_CONTINUING_SEQUENCES.md`.*

Our project sits in a real but narrow gap. Across roughly 2019 to 2026 we found **no deep-learning model that produces years of continuous 5-minute rainfall at a single rain gauge**, and **no study that trains sewer-control agents on long, statistically tested synthetic rainfall**. Learning-based rain models almost all produce rain *maps*, are steered by a coarse input (a weather forecast or a climate model), and make short pieces. The serious competition is therefore not deep learning but forty years of classical "rain-making recipes", above all the storm-and-cell models fitted to German 5-minute records and the operational German tool NiedSim3. These already produce 5-minute series of any length, cheaply. Some reproduce statistics from 5 minutes up to a decade, and NiedSim3 has been checked by running sewer simulations. Our model's real edges are three: it is a learned generator at this resolution and length, it has an unusually strict testing kit, and it is meant for training controllers. Its biggest weakness is that storms longer than about five hours are nearly missing, and one study finds that this is exactly where controllers start to behave differently. The two most important next steps are a fair comparison against a strong classical baseline and a check through the sewer model. Any novelty claim should be worded as "we found no prior work", not "first".

## How to read the evidence in this report

The notes behind this report tag every source by how well it was checked, and this report keeps those tags. A claim with no tag was confirmed on the paper itself, its abstract page, or its publisher page. **"(summary only)"** means the claim was seen only in search-result text because the publisher page was blocked; treat it as likely but unconfirmed. **"(not re-checked)"** means it comes from background knowledge and was not opened during the search; check it before citing it in the thesis. Two further warnings apply throughout. The searches were broad but not exhaustive, about twenty searches per topic. And statements that "nobody has done X" are absences in our search, not proofs.

A few terms come up repeatedly, so here they are in plain words. **Point rainfall** is rain measured at a single gauge, as opposed to a rain map. An **intensity-duration-frequency table** (engineers call it an IDF table) tells you how heavy rain gets over a given duration (say 15 minutes) for a given rarity (say once every 5 years); sewers are designed with it. **Autocorrelation** measures how much one 5-minute value resembles the next one, which is a rough measure of how "sticky" rain is. **Downscaling** means turning a coarse rain map into a sharper one. **Nowcasting** means predicting the next hour or two of rain from radar.

## Classical generators already make 5-minute rain of any length

Before deep learning, hydrologists built rainfall generators from explicit recipes with a small number of adjustable settings. Below are the five families, each described first in everyday terms.

### Storm-and-cell models: storms arrive at random and spawn bursts of rain

**The idea in everyday terms.** Picture storms arriving at random moments, like buses with no timetable. Each storm spawns a handful of "rain cells", short bursts of steady rain, each with its own strength and length. When cells overlap, their rain adds up. A small number of settings decide how often storms come, how many cells they spawn, and how strong and long the cells are. Because rain is built storm by storm, a storm always holds together: it does not fall apart into scattered single showers. The two classic versions are named after their inventors (Neyman-Scott and Bartlett-Lewis), and statisticians call the whole family "Poisson-cluster" models. A **randomised** version also lets each storm have its own typical cell length. Rodriguez-Iturbe, Cox and Isham introduced the family for rainfall, fitted to hourly Denver data ([Rodriguez-Iturbe et al. 1987](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1987.0039)), and added the randomised version a year later because the original overestimated how often periods are dry ([Rodriguez-Iturbe et al. 1988](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1988.0061)).

**Key details.** The settings are usually found by "matching averages": you adjust them until the model's mean, spread, lopsidedness, dry fraction and autocorrelation match the observed record at several time scales, usually separately for each calendar month to capture the seasons ([Northrop 2024](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10174912/1/StochasticModelsOfRainfall.pdf)). Their well-known weakness was short, intense rain: "BL models tend to underestimate hourly and sub-hourly extremes while overestimating daily extremes" ([Wei et al. 2025](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/18/1357/2025/), citing Verhoest et al. 2010). A line of work since 2014 has largely closed that gap **using German 5-minute data**. Kaczmarska, Isham and Onof compared fine-resolution variants on German 5-minute records ([Kaczmarska et al. 2014](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.4632)). Onof and Wang showed that earlier studies had restricted one setting too tightly: α, the shape of the per-storm time-scale distribution, had been held above 2 (above 4 in the older version), and the fits sat on that boundary. Once they relaxed it, the optimal α fell to 0.44-1.05 in eleven of twelve months, and the randomised Bartlett-Lewis model **clearly improved sub-hourly and hourly extremes on 69 years of 5-minute rain at Bochum, Germany**, confirmed on 105 years of 10-minute rain at Uccle, Belgium ([Onof & Wang 2020](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/24/2791/2020/)). They also showed that monthly skewness must be computed from all of a calendar month's data pooled, not averaged year by year, which underestimates it. Their model still underestimates variability beyond about a week, and they recommend pairing it with a coarse-scale model. A related model fitted to the same Bochum record reproduced the mean, spread, autocorrelation, lopsidedness and dry fraction "without any systematic bias" **from 5 minutes to a decade**, and extremes from 5 minutes to 3 days ([Kim & Onof 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022169420306107); results from a summary only; that it used the Bochum record is not confirmed). It adds a reshuffling step on top of the Onof and Wang model; without it, the randomised model reproduces monthly means but not month-to-month variability ([Wei et al. 2025](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/18/1357/2025/), Fig. 7). The leading review still adds a caution: stretching these models down to 5-minute totals for urban drainage "may not be warranted" without good sub-hourly data for checking ([Northrop 2024](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10174912/1/StochasticModelsOfRainfall.pdf)). Free software exists. **pyBL** is a Python package for the Bartlett-Lewis family that is aimed at short records ([Wei et al. 2025, GMD](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/18/1357/2025/)), and NEOPRENE does the same for Neyman-Scott ([NEOPRENE, GMD 2023](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/16/5035/2023/)). Fitting takes seconds to minutes, and simulation is almost instant.

### Cascades: split a daily total in half, again and again

**The idea in everyday terms.** Start with a known daily rain total. Split it into two halves with random shares, say 70/30. Split each half again, and keep going until you reach 5-minute steps. The total is never lost, so a long daily record can be turned into a 5-minute record.

**Key details.** The version that keeps the total exactly (the "micro-canonical" cascade) has been taken from daily to 5 minutes, but "a well-known problem of time series generated in this way is the inadequate representation of the autocorrelation" ([Müller-Thomy 2020](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/24/169/2020/)). Müller-Thomy tested fixes on 24 stations and cut the error in lag-one autocorrelation from about -4% to about 1%, with acceptable 1-5-year extremes. The method still produces too many tiny amounts, and the reshuffling step that helps is slow ([Müller-Thomy 2020](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/24/169/2020/)). Cascades also need a coarse series to start from, so making a fully synthetic 10-year record needs a second, daily generator that must be validated too.

### "Is it raining, and how much?" models, including hidden-state models

**The idea in everyday terms.** These models make two decisions at each step: first whether it is wet or dry, then how much falls. The wet/dry choice can follow a simple chain ("if it rained last step, it is likely to rain this step"), a set of hidden "weather moods" that switch over time (a hidden-state model; statisticians call it a hidden Markov model), or alternating wet and dry periods with random lengths. The amount can come from a fitted curve, a "copula" (a tool that links two random quantities so they move together in a controlled way), or resampling of real data.

**Key details.** The **alternating-renewal model** of the Hannover group describes whole events: their length, depth and internal shape. It has been "successfully applied at the 5 min timestep for urban applications" ([ESurf 2022](https://esurf.copernicus.org/articles/10/851/2022/)). A careful 2024 comparison at 10 minutes, on five German and five Korean stations, tested a copula-plus-chain model (WayDown) and a double storm-arrival model (LetItRain). Both models overestimated rain up to a threshold and underestimated it above. **Annual maxima were systematically under-predicted at the German sites.** WayDown's autocorrelation was too low beyond one step ([Vorobevskii et al. 2024](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/391/2024/)). **CoSMoS** takes a smooth, bell-curve-shaped random process and bends it into the shape of rainfall. It can match "any probability distribution and any linear autocorrelation structure" ([CoSMoS documentation](https://rdrr.io/cran/CoSMoS/man/CoSMoS-package.html); [Papalexiou 2018](https://water.usask.ca/resources/cosmos.php)). CoSMoS is the published cousin of our own simple baseline, which also bends a bell-curve process into rainfall. Hidden-state models of rainfall exist mainly at hourly or daily resolution, for example a Bayesian hidden-state model for **hourly** urban-flood rainfall trained on 8 years of data ([Stoner & Economou 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.03846)). Our project uses hidden states only as a label that steers the diffusion model, so it does not compete with this line directly.

### Resampling: cut and reuse pieces of the real record

**The idea in everyday terms.** Take a day's rainfall total from a generator, find real days with similar totals, and borrow the hour-by-hour pattern of one of them. The shapes are always realistic because they are real.

**Key details.** This is called the "method of fragments". It is usually paired with "nearest-neighbour" matching, which picks the most similar real days ([Westra et al. 2012](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2011WR010489); [Li et al. 2018](https://rmets.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/joc.5438)). In a comparison of daily-to-sub-daily methods, only the method of fragments matched observed hourly IDF behaviour, while cascades mis-estimated extremes ([Pui et al. 2012](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022169412007202); summary only). Pyraingen packages these Australian methods in Python ([Pyraingen 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1364815224000458)). The obvious limitation is that resampling cannot create a pattern that never occurred.

### German practice: KOSTRA tables and the NiedSim3 generator

**The idea in everyday terms.** German engineers mainly use two tools. **KOSTRA-DWD** is the German Weather Service's official map of how heavy rain gets for each duration and rarity, on a 5 km grid, starting at 5 minutes. **NiedSim3** is a state-distributed program that makes synthetic 5-minute rain for any 1 km grid cell.

**Key details.** KOSTRA-DWD-2020 has been the valid version since 1 January 2023 ([DWD user guide](https://dwd.de/DE/leistungen/kostra_dwd_rasterwerte/download/kostra_dwd_2020_anwenderhilfe_pdf.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=3)). Design storms built from it are fine for sizing pipes. Overflow assessment for combined sewers, where rainwater and wastewater share pipes, needs **continuous long-term simulation**, because overflows happen several times a month and depend on how full the tanks still are from earlier rain ([Müller, Mosthaf & Bárdossy 2018](https://doi.bafg.de/HyWa/2018/HyWa_2018,4_1.pdf)). NiedSim has been distributed in Baden-Württemberg since 2000 and in Hesse, Rhineland-Palatinate and Bavaria since 2003-2009. The current NiedSim3 works in four stages ([Müller et al. 2018](https://doi.bafg.de/HyWa/2018/HyWa_2018,4_1.pdf)). First, it pools statistics from **1,034 five-minute, 1,551 hourly and 5,468 daily stations** onto a 1 km grid. Second, it draws hourly values year by year, pinning about 80% of big hours in place so that they cannot merge into unrealistic mega-storms. Third, it reshuffles hours, then 5-minute slices within each hour, until autocorrelations, wet-spell lengths and scaling match. Fourth, it **rescales the extremes to match the KOSTRA table** without changing the timing. Its validation is the standard our project should aim for: **sewer simulations driven by NiedSim3 rain fell within the natural sampling uncertainty of simulations driven by real rain**, although there were systematic deviations in hilly regions ([Müller et al. 2018](https://doi.bafg.de/HyWa/2018/HyWa_2018,4_1.pdf)). We did not check whether researchers can obtain NiedSim outside state engineering use.

| Family | How it works, in one line | Main strength | Main weakness at 5 minutes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Storm-and-cell (Bartlett-Lewis, Neyman-Scott) | Random storms spawn overlapping bursts of rain | Storms hold together by design; any length; cheap; modern versions fit 5-min extremes | Older versions miss short extremes; settings found by matching chosen averages only |
| Cascades | Split a daily total in half again and again | Keeps totals exactly; cheap | Rain too "unsticky"; too many tiny amounts; needs a daily series |
| Wet/dry then amount (chains, hidden states, copulas, renewal) | Decide wet or dry, then how much | Flexible; event-based versions describe whole storms | German annual maxima under-predicted in a 2024 test |
| Resampling | Reuse real patterns | Shapes always realistic | Cannot invent new patterns |
| NiedSim3 (German practice) | Reshuffle until statistics match, then rescale extremes to KOSTRA | Built for German sewers; checked through sewer simulation | Rests on interpolated statistics; deviations in hilly areas |

## Deep-learning rain models mostly sharpen or forecast maps

**The idea in everyday terms.** A deep generative model learns to make new examples that look like its training data, without anyone writing down a recipe. There are four main kinds. A **GAN** (generative adversarial network) is a forger and a detective trained against each other until the forgeries fool the detective. A **VAE** (variational autoencoder) learns to squeeze data into a short code and rebuild it; feeding it new random codes produces new data. A **diffusion model**, which is what we use, learns to remove noise a little at a time, so that starting from pure static it can "clean up" into a new rainfall record. A **normalising flow**, and its newer cousin flow matching, learns a smooth, reversible warp from simple randomness to data. A **transformer** learns to predict the next piece from the pieces so far.

### What the field actually builds

Most of this literature does not do what we do. The largest group is **downscaling**: turning a coarse rain map into a sharp one. These models first used GANs and, from about 2023, diffusion. Examples include a GAN versus VAE-GAN comparison ([Harris et al. 2022](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2022MS003120); author list not re-checked), a diffusion emulator of UK convection-resolving climate runs at daily resolution ([Addison et al. 2026](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2025MS005140); summary only), and RainScaleGAN ([AIES 2025](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/aies/4/3/AIES-D-24-0074.1.xml)). The second group is **nowcasting** radar maps a short time ahead, for example the GAN behind DeepMind's nowcaster DGMR (not re-checked) and latent diffusion in LDCast ([Leinonen et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.12891)). The only **sub-hourly** deep generative rain found is gridded and steered by a coarse input. spateGAN-ERA5 turns a global hourly weather reanalysis into **2 km, 10-minute** rain maps. It was trained on Germany alone and still worked in the United States and Australia ([Glawion et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16098)). This is the most relevant deep model we found, but its storm timing is copied from the reanalysis, so it does not invent new weather.

Generators of synthetic records, which is our task, are few:

| Study | What it makes | How it differs from us |
|---|---|---|
| [Ji et al. 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1364815223002827) (summary only) | GAN; **30 years of hourly rain at 14 stations**, fed into a river model for flood frequency | Hourly, not 5-min; how the 30 years are assembled is unknown (paywalled) |
| [Guevara et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.08074) | GAN and VAE against classical weather generators, multisite | Called "preliminary" by the authors; daily-style multisite data |
| [DiffESM, Bassetti et al. 2024](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023MS004194) (summary only) | Diffusion; daily rain maps from monthly averages | Long-term coherence comes from the monthly input, not learned |
| [cBottle 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06474) (summary only) | Global diffusion "climate in a bottle" down to 5 km | Snapshots of the atmosphere, not a gauge series |
| [Bakhshipour et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.13733) | Bayesian GAN making rain and tank inflow for a small German town, to help predict sewer flows | Short series used for data augmentation, not long records or control |

Our model fits the overall trend, which moved from GANs (2019-2022) to GAN-plus-extreme-statistics hybrids (2021-2023) and then to diffusion (2023 onward). We found little on flows or transformers as long-record rain generators. Flow matching appears in downscaling ([arXiv 2606.00281](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.00281); summary only), and transformers appear in nowcasting, for example BlockGPT, which predicts radar frame by frame ([arXiv 2510.06293](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.06293); summary only).

### The recurring weaknesses are zeros, extremes and long storms

Three problems keep reappearing, and our project meets all three. **Exact zeros** come first. A GAN that turned daily totals into hourly rain "cannot produce exactly zero precipitation values" and made too many light-rain hours. Adding season inputs even made it *worse* ([Scher & Peßenteiner 2021](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/3207/2021/)). **Extremes** come second. The motivation for ExGAN was that plain GANs "generate typical rather than extreme samples" ([Bhatia et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.08454); summary only). The evtGAN hybrid captured rain extremes less well than temperature extremes ([Boulaguiem et al. 2022](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/environmental-data-science/article/modeling-and-simulating-spatial-extremes-by-combining-extreme-value-theory-with-generative-adversarial-networks/AA3CEBA3CF1EF3C9C8D182C108289D7F); summary only). A 2026 intercomparison found that a plain prediction network underestimates the heaviest rain, while GANs and diffusion get closer ([GMD 2026](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/19/7545/2026/); summary only). **Long storms** come third, and they are the least studied. **Nearly every model makes fixed-size pieces**: one snapshot, one day, one month, or a short radar clip. Long records come either from a coarse driver (monthly averages, a reanalysis) or from feeding outputs back in. **We found no paper that measures how storm lengths survive the joins between pieces**, and no rain paper that builds multi-year 5-minute point records from independent pieces as we do.

General time-series research does offer ways to join pieces. WaveStitch generates pieces side by side and aligns their overlaps, and reports being 166 times faster than one-piece-at-a-time generation ([WaveStitch 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.06231)). CompDiffuser makes each piece while looking at its neighbours ([CompDiffuser 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.05153)). Neither has been applied to rain. Our companion note covers the fill-in trick we borrowed from photo repair, which redoes each clean-up step several times so that the new part blends with the given part. That trick is **known to be biased** and to drift towards what is most common in the training data (`docs/LITERATURE_CONTINUING_SEQUENCES.md`). This fits our finding that context-chained generation came out drier.

On how rainfall is reshaped before training: the usual choice is "log of one plus rain" followed by rescaling ([arXiv 2512.13987](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.13987)). A 2026 paper argues that a "soft log" squashes heavy tails enough for standard flow models to work ([Tail Annealing, arXiv 2605.20068](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.20068); summary only). Our asinh transform behaves the same way: straight near zero and logarithmic for heavy rain. **We found no rainfall paper using asinh.** Heavier-tailed noise is another recent idea, replacing bell-curve noise with a fatter-tailed kind ([Heavy-Tailed Diffusion, arXiv 2410.14171](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.14171); summary only, authors not re-checked).

## Simulation-based calibration barely touches rainfall yet

**The idea in everyday terms.** Suppose you have a rain-making recipe with a few knobs, such as how often storms come or how strong cells are. You cannot write down a formula for "how likely is the real record under these knob settings", but you can run the recipe. Simulation-based inference (often shortened to SBI) uses only this ability to run it. The older version, **approximate Bayesian computation**, tries many knob settings, simulates rain for each, and keeps the settings whose simulated statistics (dry fraction, spell lengths, autocorrelation) come close to the real ones. The surviving settings show both the best values and how uncertain they are. The newer **neural** version trains a network that looks at a set of statistics and directly suggests plausible knob settings. Once trained, it can be reused instantly for new targets.

**Key details.** Only one group has fitted storm-and-cell rain models this way. Aryal and Jones fitted a Bartlett-Lewis model to **6-minute** Australian gauge data and reported that their method "gives a better fit" than the usual average-matching. They described it as apparently "the first time ABC has been used to fit rainfall models" ([Aryal & Jones 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378475419303313); abstract only, so the size of the improvement is unconfirmed). They then extended the approach to radar rain maps ([Aryal & Jones 2021](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10174912/1/StochasticModelsOfRainfall.pdf), seen through the Northrop review). The real gain is freedom: you can fit to statistics such as spell-length distributions that have no neat formula ([Northrop 2024](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10174912/1/StochasticModelsOfRainfall.pdf)).

The one neural example for weather is recent and German. Groenke and co-authors used the `sbi` software to calibrate a **daily** temperature-and-rain generator on German Weather Service data for Potsdam. Each round drew 1,000 knob settings, each simulating a 50-year series, and a run took **10-30 minutes**. Their aim was "climatology matching": finding settings that reproduce *deliberately shifted* target statistics, so that impact models can be stress-tested under changed climates. They call it "one of the first applications of SBI to the domain of weather and climate simulation" ([Groenke et al. 2026](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2025JH000902); [full text](https://publications.pik-potsdam.de/rest/items/item_34299_1/component/file_34300/content)). In hydrology, neural simulation-based inference has calibrated a watershed model through a fast stand-in network ([Hull et al. 2024](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/4685/2024/)). The approximate version has calibrated urban runoff and pollution models ([Chowdhury et al. 2022](https://iwaponline.com/wst/article/86/2/321/89618/Automatic-model-calibration-of-combined-hydrologic)).

**What this means for us.** We found no simulation-based calibration of a sub-daily rain generator with the neural method, none applied *to* a deep generative rain model, and none combined with reinforcement-learning control of water systems. A diffusion model is the opposite design choice. It has no small set of meaningful knobs; it learns the whole distribution of 5-minute sequences directly. That spares us from choosing which statistics matter. It also means the model spends effort on every feature in proportion to how common it is, which helps explain why rare extremes are under-fitted. And it gives us **no direct way to ask for "the same climate but with 15% heavier short bursts"**, which is exactly what Groenke's approach provides. Our only steering handle is the hidden-state label.

## Rainfall papers test with averages; few check the sewer or the controller

**Classical evaluation.** Rain-generator papers report a stable set of checks: averages and spread across time scales, dry fraction, autocorrelation, wet and dry spell lengths, extreme values and IDF tables ([Northrop 2024](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10174912/1/StochasticModelsOfRainfall.pdf); [Vorobevskii et al. 2024](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/391/2024/)). There is **no agreed standard or benchmark dataset**. The NiedSim3 authors note that checking through sewer simulation is recommended but rare, and that acceptable deviations should be measured against the natural year-to-year variation of real records ([Müller et al. 2018](https://doi.bafg.de/HyWa/2018/HyWa_2018,4_1.pdf)). The 2024 German/Korean study did not run any sewer model and called for one as future work ([Vorobevskii et al. 2024](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/391/2024/)).

**Machine-learning evaluation.** Generic time-series generation uses a different toolkit. One check is a **classifier two-sample test**: train a classifier to tell real from fake, where a coin-flip result is good ([Lopez-Paz & Oquab 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.06545); not re-checked). Another is **precision, recall and coverage**: are the fakes realistic, and do they cover all kinds of real data ([Naeem et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09797), not re-checked; [Alaa et al. 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/alaa22a/alaa22a.pdf)). A third is **train on synthetic, test on real**: does a model trained on fake data work on real data ([Yoon et al. 2019](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2019/hash/c9efe5f26cd17ba6216bbe2a7d26d490-Abstract.html); not re-checked). A fourth is **memorisation checks**: is the generator just copying training data ([Alaa et al. 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/alaa22a/alaa22a.pdf)). Benchmarks warn that these scores can be unstable, because they depend on retraining randomly initialised networks, and that their definitions are often ambiguous ([TSGBench 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.03755)). **We found no rainfall-generator paper using any of these four, and none using a real-versus-real yardstick from held-out years.**

**Downstream use in drainage and control.** Classical drainage work does compare synthetic and observed rain through sewer models, judging overflow volume, peak overflow and the number of active overflows ([J. Hydrology 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022169417304390); summary only). Continuous simulation is preferred over single design storms for sizing overflow structures ([J. Hydrology 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022169418306668); summary only). Reinforcement-learning papers on drainage, by contrast, train on **a few dozen discrete storms**. Saliba et al. selected flood-causing events from 10 years of data in Norfolk, Virginia ([Saliba et al. 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/12/11/3222)). One group reportedly used 50 designed storms ([Tian et al. 2022](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2021WR030703); event count from a summary only). Work on our own benchmark, Astlingen, reportedly sampled **50 events from the 2000-2006 5-minute record** for training. Which Astlingen paper this was is uncertain, because both candidate pages were blocked ([J. Hydrology 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022169423003773); [multi-agent study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366587915_Multi-agent_reinforcement_learning_applications_in_the_real-time_control_of_an_urban_drainage_system)). A 2025 study states plainly that "due to the scarcity of long-term rainfall data, synthetic rainfall events are used" ([npj Clean Water 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41545-025-00512-z); summary only).

The most important single finding for our project comes from Huang et al. (2025). For storms **shorter than 5 hours**, a controller that plans ahead with a forecast and a sewer model (model predictive control) had the lowest flooding risk. **For longer storms its performance "rapidly declined"**, while the learning-based controller kept beating simple rule-based control regardless of storm length ([Huang et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11850747/)). Storm length is therefore not a side statistic: **it is the very property that reorders controllers, and it is where our model is weakest.**

## Where we have an edge

Each point below rests on searches that were broad but not exhaustive, so each should be worded as "we found no prior work" in the thesis.

**A learned generator at an unclaimed resolution and length.** We found no deep generative model that produces multi-year continuous 5-minute rain at a gauge. The nearest deep models are hourly (Ji et al. 2024), gridded and steered by a coarse input (spateGAN-ERA5), or short (Bakhshipour et al.). The classical literature does occupy this space, so our edge is "learned rather than hand-built", not "the only 5-minute generator".

**Rain made for training controllers.** Reinforcement-learning drainage studies train on tens of storms. Training on **ten continuous years**, with realistic dry gaps, tanks still partly full from earlier rain, and storms in quick succession, appears to be new. The evidence notes suggest these "tanks not yet empty" effects are under-studied in control work, and continuous series exercise them where storm-by-storm training cannot.

**A stricter testing kit than either field uses alone.** Our 18 acceptance checks cover the classical rainfall statistics. On top of them we add a real-versus-real yardstick from held-out years, which tells us which mismatches exceed natural year-to-year variation. We also add the four machine-learning checks described above, none of which we found in rainfall work. NiedSim3's use of bootstrap uncertainty bands is the closest classical analogue, and it is worth citing as precedent rather than claiming the idea outright.

**Clear wins over simple statistical baselines.** Our best model (asinh transform) matches 12 of 15 IDF cells, against 0 of 15 for the bell-curve-based baselines, and shows no sign of copying training data. This is real, but see the first point under "behind": those baselines are weak.

**Two small methods findings.** We found no rainfall paper using the asinh transform; our comparison, in which asinh and log versions tie at 14 of 18 checks and both beat plain rescaling, fits the soft-log argument of the Tail Annealing paper. We also found no report that the photo-repair fill-in trick dries out mostly-zero data such as rain. Both are modest contributions and should be presented as such.

## Where we are behind

**Long storms, the property that matters most for control.** Real records have about 14 storms a year longer than one 5 hour 20 minute block (64 steps of 5 minutes); our records have about 2. Storm-and-cell models get storm coherence for free, and NiedSim3 explicitly matches wet-spell lengths. Huang et al. show that storms over 5 hours are where controllers diverge. Context chaining, where each block starts from the end of the previous one, closed the gap but halved the rainfall. A pilot without the step-redo trick keeps the wetness, but it is not yet scored.

**Our baselines are too weak to impress a hydrology reviewer.** The first bell-curve baselines broke rain into single-step showers (75% of them). That was their fit: refitted so the bell-curve process rains as persistently as real rain (October 2026), the copula baseline gets showers and long storms right, but makes heavy rain last too long (1-hour to 1-day extremes 1.3 to 1.9 times real). A modern randomised Bartlett-Lewis model, fitted to German 5-minute data, reproduces 5- and 10-minute extremes ([Onof & Wang 2020](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/24/2791/2020/)), and an extended version reproduces statistics from 5 minutes to a decade ([Kim & Onof 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022169420306107)). It is the comparison a reviewer will ask for, and pyBL makes it cheap.

**Much less data.** We train on 8 years at one gauge. The Bochum benchmark has 69 years, and NiedSim3 pools over a thousand 5-minute stations. With 8 years, the rarest IDF cells are themselves uncertain, which our held-out yardstick partly accounts for.

**No handle on extremes or climate change.** NiedSim3 rescales extremes to the official KOSTRA table and can swap in a future table. Classical models use change factors or temperature scaling ([Vorobevskii et al. 2024](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/391/2024/)). Simulation-based calibration can steer a generator toward any target climate ([Groenke et al. 2026](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2025JH000902)). We can only steer by hidden-state label, and 3 of 15 IDF cells still fall outside the accepted band.

**No sewer check yet.** NiedSim3 was validated by running the sewer model; we have not run real and synthetic rain through Astlingen and compared the overflows.

**Single site, slow, opaque.** Classical generators fit in minutes, simulate instantly, have interpretable settings and, with Bayesian fitting, report uncertainty about those settings. Our model needs cluster training, cannot be read off, and gives no uncertainty about the rain regime itself.

## What to do next

1. **Build a strong classical baseline.** Fit a randomised Bartlett-Lewis model with pyBL on the same 2000-2007 training years and score it on the same 18 checks, the long-storm count and the IDF table. If it wins on long storms, say so; the diffusion model's case then rests on other statistics and on shape realism.
2. **Place the model against German practice.** Compare our IDF table with KOSTRA-DWD-2020 for the grid cell of the gauge behind the Astlingen record (confirm the gauge's location first). Ask whether NiedSim3 series can be obtained for research (licence unverified); if not, discuss it as the practice standard.
3. **Run the sewer check before the controller check.** Push real and synthetic 10-year series through Astlingen in SWMM (the stormwater simulation software) under fixed control. Compare overflow volume, overflow frequency, flooding and tank-full durations against the real-versus-real yardstick. This is the established classical test and reuses our existing environment. Training controllers on synthetic rain and testing them on real years remains the later, novel step.
4. **Fix long storms, then measure storms over 5 hours specifically.** Score the no-redo chaining pilot first. If it falls short, try overlap-aligned joining (WaveStitch, CompDiffuser) or a two-level design in which a coarse series (for example 6-hourly totals) is made first and then filled in at 5 minutes, the pattern used by DiffESM and Scher & Peßenteiner.
5. **Consider a NiedSim-style extreme correction.** Rescaling the heaviest bursts to an IDF target after generation, without changing timing, is a cheap, published idea. Fatter-tailed noise is a heavier option.
6. **Word novelty cautiously.** For example: "we found no prior deep generative model producing multi-year continuous 5-minute point rainfall", "we found no prior reinforcement-learning drainage study trained on long continuous synthetic rainfall", and "we found no prior use of classifier two-sample tests, precision/recall or memorisation checks for rainfall generators". Avoid "first".
7. **Verify shaky sources before citing them.** These include Ji et al. 2024 (how its 30 years are assembled), which Astlingen paper used 50 events, the event count in Tian et al. 2022, the author list for the "5 minutes to a decade" paper, and the machine-learning metric papers marked "not re-checked".
8. **Keep steering as future work.** Treating the hidden-state plan or an intensity scale as knobs and using simulation-based calibration to hit shifted targets (Groenke's "climatology matching") would let us stress-test controllers under heavier rain. We found no precedent for doing this with a deep generator.

## Conclusion

This review changes what the thesis should compare against. The natural rival is not another neural network, since there is almost none at this resolution and length. It is a well-fitted storm-and-cell model and the German NiedSim3/KOSTRA practice, both of which already make credible 5-minute rain for decades at negligible cost. The defensible contribution is therefore a combination: a learned generator, tested more strictly than either the hydrology or the machine-learning literature tests rainfall, built specifically to supply continuous training years to controllers. Each part has precedent somewhere; we found none that combines them.

The second shift is in priorities. Long storms looked like one failed statistic among eighteen. The control literature suggests they are the statistic that decides which controller looks best, so a generator that drops them could quietly bias the very agents it is meant to train. Fixing long storms and running the sewer-level comparison matter more for the thesis than further gains on the averages. A head-to-head with Bartlett-Lewis will show whether the diffusion model earns its cost.
